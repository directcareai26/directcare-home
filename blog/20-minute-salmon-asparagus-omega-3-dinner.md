## 20-Minute Salmon and Asparagus: Hit Your Omega-3 Target

 A single 6-ounce serving of wild sockeye salmon delivers roughly 1.5–2 g of EPA+DHA — enough to meet most clinical omega-3 targets in one meal. Here's the 20-minute sheet-pan version, plus what the numbers on your plate actually do inside your body.

 Most "healthy salmon dinners" online are written for photos, not for physiology. The question worth asking isn't whether salmon is good for you — it's whether the portion you're eating actually delivers the EPA+DHA dose associated with clinical benefit.

 This recipe is built backward from that number. Twenty minutes, one sheet pan, and a portion sized to move the needle on the omega-3 index most cardiology guidelines care about.

### Why the omega-3 dose matters, in plain English

 EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid) are the two long-chain omega-3s your cell membranes actually use. The American Heart Association recommends roughly 1 g/day of combined EPA+DHA for people with established coronary disease, and 2 servings of fatty fish weekly for general prevention (Siscovick et al., Circulation, 2017).

 Higher doses — 2 to 4 g/day — have shown triglyceride reductions of 25–30% in randomized trials (Skulas-Ray et al., Circulation, 2019). The **Omega-3 Index**, which measures EPA+DHA as a percentage of red blood cell fatty acids, is the biomarker worth watching: a target of ≥8% is associated with lower cardiovascular mortality (Harris et al., J Clin Lipidol, 2018).

 Here's the practical translation:

- Farmed Atlantic salmon (6 oz): ~1.8–2.2 g EPA+DHA

- Wild sockeye (6 oz): ~1.5–1.9 g EPA+DHA

- Wild coho (6 oz): ~1.2–1.5 g EPA+DHA

- Canned pink salmon (6 oz): ~1.0–1.3 g EPA+DHA

 Two salmon dinners a week — this one and one other — gets most adults to the AHA's prevention target without a capsule.

### The 20-minute sheet-pan salmon and asparagus

 **Serves 2. Total time: 20 minutes.**

#### Ingredients

- 2 salmon fillets, 6 oz each, skin-on (wild sockeye or farmed Atlantic)

- 1 lb asparagus, tough ends snapped off

- 2 tbsp extra-virgin olive oil, divided

- 1 lemon (half juiced, half sliced)

- 3 garlic cloves, thinly sliced

- 1 tsp Dijon mustard

- 1 tsp honey or maple syrup

- ½ tsp kosher salt

- ¼ tsp black pepper

- ¼ tsp smoked paprika

- Optional: 2 tbsp chopped fresh dill or parsley

#### Method

 1. **Preheat and prep.** Set the oven to 425°F (220°C). Line a sheet pan with parchment. Pat the salmon dry — dry surface is the difference between a crust and a steam bath. 2. **Dress the asparagus.** Toss spears with 1 tbsp olive oil, half the garlic, ¼ tsp salt, and pepper. Spread on one side of the pan. 3. **Glaze the salmon.** Whisk the remaining olive oil, Dijon, honey, lemon juice, remaining garlic, smoked paprika, and remaining salt. Brush over the salmon fillets. Place skin-side down on the pan next to the asparagus. Lay lemon slices on top. 4. **Roast.** Bake 10–12 minutes, until the salmon flakes at the thickest point and reads 125–130°F internal for medium (it will carry over to 130–135°F, the FDA-endorsed doneness range for finfish). 5. **Rest and finish.** Let rest 2 minutes. Shower with fresh dill or parsley. Serve.

#### Nutrition per serving (6 oz sockeye + half the asparagus)

- Calories: ~430

- Protein: 38 g

- Fat: 26 g (of which ~1.7 g EPA+DHA)

- Carbohydrates: 12 g

- Fiber: 4 g

 {callout: The number that matters} One 6-oz serving of fatty salmon delivers 1.5–2 g EPA+DHA — roughly two days' worth of the AHA prevention target in a single meal.

### What actually changes on your labs

 Regular fatty fish intake (2+ servings/week) shifts several markers over 8–12 weeks:

- Triglycerides: typically drop 10–20% at dietary doses; 25–30% at 2–4 g/day supplemental EPA+DHA (Skulas-Ray et al., 2019).

- Omega-3 Index: rises approximately 1–2 percentage points per 1 g/day EPA+DHA over 3 months (Flock et al., J Am Heart Assoc , 2013).

- hs-CRP: modest reductions in some trials, inconsistent in others. Don't expect a large signal from diet alone.

- Blood pressure: small reductions (~2–3 mmHg systolic) at higher intakes (Miller et al., Am J Hypertens , 2014).

 These aren't dramatic on any single lab, but stacked with resistance training, adequate protein, and reasonable sleep, they add up. If you want to know where your Omega-3 Index actually sits, it's one of the more useful add-ons on a lipid panel — the markers worth having before you decide whether to supplement are your Omega-3 Index, triglycerides, and hs-CRP.

 Food-first is a reasonable strategy for omega-3s — but only if the portion size matches the pharmacology.

### Four variations, tuned to your goals

 **1. Higher-protein cut (for training days).** Bump salmon to 8 oz and add 1 cup cooked quinoa on the side. Now you're at ~52 g protein and ~2.4 g EPA+DHA per plate. Useful on lifting days if you're anchoring dinner around recovery.

 **2. Lower-carb version.** Skip the honey in the glaze and swap in ½ tsp Dijon plus a pinch of chili flake. Serves well over a bed of arugula dressed with olive oil and lemon. Drops the meal to ~7 g net carbs.

 **3. Mediterranean upgrade.** Add ½ cup halved cherry tomatoes and 10 kalamata olives to the pan for the last 8 minutes. Finishes with ~5 g fiber and additional monounsaturated fat — the same fatty acid profile studied in PREDIMED (Estruch et al., N Engl J Med, 2018).

 **4. Meal-prep batch (4 servings).** Double everything, use 4 fillets on a larger sheet pan, and cook asparagus separately for 8 minutes so it doesn't overcook when reheated. Salmon reheats best at 275°F for 8 minutes, covered — high heat turns it dry and fishy.

### A few clinical notes worth mentioning

 **Mercury.** Salmon — wild or farmed — is a low-mercury fish per FDA guidance and safe at 2–3 servings per week for adults, including during pregnancy.

 **Farmed vs. wild.** Farmed Atlantic salmon is actually slightly higher in total EPA+DHA per ounce because of its higher fat content, though it also carries more omega-6. Wild sockeye has a better omega-3-to-omega-6 ratio and more astaxanthin. Both are reasonable choices; the one you'll actually cook twice a week beats the one you buy occasionally out of guilt.

 **Supplements.** If you don't eat fish, an algae- or fish-oil-based EPA+DHA supplement providing 1–2 g/day is the standard evidence-based alternative. Look for third-party tested products (IFOS, USP) and check the actual EPA+DHA content — not just "fish oil 1000 mg," which usually contains only 300 mg of the actives.

 **Anticoagulants.** High-dose omega-3 supplementation (>3 g/day) can modestly increase bleeding time. If you're on warfarin, apixaban, or antiplatelet therapy, dietary salmon is fine — but talk to a clinician before layering supplements.
