## Compounded Semaglutide Online: what to verify first

 Compounded semaglutide is available through many online platforms, but quality and oversight vary widely. Here is what to check about the pharmacy, the clinician, and the follow-up plan before you hand over a credit card.

 Before signing up for compounded semaglutide online, verify the pharmacy is a licensed 503A or 503B facility in good standing, confirm a licensed clinician in your state is reviewing your intake, and understand how dose titration, side-effect management, and follow-up are handled over the months you may be on therapy.

 Compounded semaglutide moved from a niche workaround during the FDA shortage listing to a mainstream telehealth offering almost overnight. The branded injectables (Wegovy, Ozempic) worked in trials like STEP 1, where adults on 2.4 mg weekly lost on average 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks versus 2.4% on placebo. Patients want that effect at a lower cash price, and compounded versions filled the gap.

 But "compounded semaglutide online" is not one product from one supply chain. It is a label that covers everything from well-run 503B outsourcing facilities with sterile injectable experience to pop-up websites with a logo and a Stripe account. The FDA has flagged adverse events tied to unapproved GLP-1 products, including dosing errors from unfamiliar vial concentrations. Before you sign up, there are specific things worth verifying.

### Verify the pharmacy, not just the website

 The website you buy from is almost never the pharmacy that makes the drug. Ask — in writing if you have to — which pharmacy fills the prescription, and then check that pharmacy directly.

 Two categories matter under federal law:

- 503A compounding pharmacies make patient-specific prescriptions. They are regulated primarily by state boards of pharmacy.

- 503B outsourcing facilities can make larger batches and are inspected by the FDA under cGMP standards closer to those for conventional manufacturers.

 Neither category produces an FDA-approved finished product — compounded drugs are not reviewed for safety, efficacy, or quality the way Wegovy or Ozempic are. That is a trade-off, not a disqualifier, but it is the trade-off. What you want to confirm:

- The pharmacy's name, state license number, and status on that state's board of pharmacy website.

- Whether it is 503A or 503B, and whether it has recent FDA warning letters (searchable at fda.gov).

- Whether the semaglutide is being compounded as the base molecule or as a salt form (sodium, acetate). The FDA has stated that salt forms are different active ingredients than the semaglutide in approved products and raise additional safety questions.

 If a telehealth brand will not tell you which pharmacy fills your prescription, that is the answer.

### Verify the clinician, in your state

 A prescription for an injectable GLP-1 is a medical decision that should be made by a clinician licensed in the state where you live. "Licensed somewhere in the US" is not the standard — telemedicine prescribing is governed state by state.

 What to confirm before you pay:

- The prescribing clinician's full name and license type (MD, DO, NP, PA).

- That the license is active in your state, verifiable on your state medical or nursing board site.

- That there is a real intake — medical history, current medications, contraindications like personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2, pregnancy status, pancreatitis history, gastroparesis — not just a checkbox form that routes to approval.

 A thorough workup for a GLP-1 would typically include a review of baseline weight and vitals, relevant history, current medications (especially insulin or sulfonylureas, where hypoglycemia risk changes), and labs when they add decision-useful information — A1c, kidney function, lipids, and liver enzymes are the common ones. If you choose to run bloodwork, those are reasonable markers to have on file. Labs are not the gate; clinical review of the whole picture is.

### Verify the follow-up plan, before you need it

 GLP-1 therapy is not a one-shot transaction. Semaglutide is titrated slowly — the labeled schedule for Wegovy starts at 0.25 mg weekly and steps up roughly every four weeks toward a 2.4 mg maintenance dose, specifically to reduce nausea, vomiting, and GI side effects. Compounded protocols should follow published titration ranges, not accelerate them to "get results faster."

 Ask the platform:

- How often does a clinician actually reassess the dose, and through what channel?

- What happens if side effects (persistent nausea, vomiting, signs of pancreatitis, gallbladder symptoms) show up on a Saturday night?

- Is there a path to pause, de-escalate, or discontinue — and is that path free, or does it cost another visit fee each time?

- What is the plan for maintenance after weight loss plateaus, which the STEP trials showed typically occurs around month 9–12?

 A GLP-1 program without a real follow-up loop is a vending machine with a stethoscope sticker on it.
 **The single most important check**If the platform cannot tell you the name of the pharmacy, the name and state license of the prescribing clinician, and how dose changes are handled after month one — those three things — keep shopping.

### Red flags worth taking seriously

 Some patterns correlate with the problems the FDA has flagged in its communications on unapproved GLP-1s:

- Dosing in "units" on an insulin syringe without a clear mg conversion. Dosing errors have led to hospitalizations.

- Marketing that promises a specific pound or percentage loss. The STEP and SURMOUNT trials report averages with wide ranges; individuals vary substantially.

- Combination products (semaglutide plus B12, plus L-carnitine, plus anything else) marketed as superior. There is no trial evidence those combinations outperform semaglutide alone, and they complicate adverse-event attribution.

- No written protocol for what happens if you develop severe abdominal pain, signs of pancreatitis, or vision changes.

- A pharmacy name that changes between the sales page and the shipping label.

### What a reasonable online GLP-1 program looks like

 In plain English: a licensed clinician in your state reviews your history and relevant labs when available, prescribes at a titration schedule consistent with the published label range, works with a disclosed, licensed compounding pharmacy, and stays reachable for the months the medication is in your body. Side effects get addressed by a human, not a FAQ page. Dose changes are documented. Discontinuation is as easy as starting.

 Compounded semaglutide can be a legitimate option during a documented shortage or for specific clinical reasons. It is not an FDA-approved finished product, and the quality of the experience depends almost entirely on the pharmacy filling it and the clinician behind it. Those are the two names worth knowing before you sign up.
