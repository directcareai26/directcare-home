## Creatine monohydrate for women: the cognitive, bone, and longevity case (it's not just for the gym)

 The cleanest, cheapest, best-studied supplement on the market is also the one most women have written off as a bodybuilding drug. The published evidence in cognition, bone density, and healthy aging says they shouldn't.

 Creatine has a marketing problem. The bottles look like supplements aimed at college-age men. The label imagery features people deadlifting. The internet calls it a "performance enhancer."

 What it actually is: **the single most well-studied supplement in the published nutrition literature**, with the strongest safety record of any oral supplement on the market, and a body of evidence — particularly in women, particularly past 40 — that goes far beyond muscle.

 Here's the clinical case for creatine in women, what the published data actually shows, and how a typical protocol fits with the rest of what we prescribe.

### What creatine actually is.

 Creatine is a small molecule your body already makes, primarily in the liver and kidneys, and stores mostly in muscle (~95%) and brain (~5%). Its job is to recycle ATP — the cellular energy currency — fast enough to support short bursts of high-energy demand. Muscle contractions are the most obvious example. Neurons also use ATP heavily, and that's where the cognitive story starts.

 Your body makes about 1 gram a day. Diet (red meat, fish) provides another 1–2 grams. Supplementation adds 3–5 grams. The result: your muscle and brain creatine stores get more saturated than diet alone can achieve.

 **Women, on average, have lower baseline creatine stores than men.** Lower dietary intake (less red meat) plus higher endogenous turnover means most women are starting further from saturation. This is a meaningful part of why supplementation often produces bigger relative effects in women than in men.

### The cognitive case.

 Multiple controlled trials and a [2024 Cochrane-style systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37879417/) have looked at creatine and cognition. The headline findings:

- Memory: creatine supplementation produces small but consistent improvements in short-term memory tasks across healthy adults, with larger effects in older adults and in vegetarians/vegans (who have lower baseline creatine).

- Mental fatigue under stress: the effect is most pronounced in conditions where the brain is taxed — sleep deprivation, cognitively demanding tasks, perimenopausal cognitive symptoms.

- Mood: several studies have shown adjunctive creatine improves outcomes in depression, particularly in women, though this is still emerging.

- Concussion and TBI recovery: small studies suggest faster recovery in patients on creatine.

 The brain mechanism

 Neurons rely on rapid ATP cycling exactly the way muscle cells do. Higher brain creatine stores buffer the energy demands of demanding cognitive work — particularly in conditions where the brain is under-fueled (sleep deprivation, mental fatigue, hormonal flux). The cognitive benefit isn't a side effect; it's a parallel mechanism.

### The bone case (this is the biggest one for women past 40).

 Perimenopause and menopause drive accelerated bone loss in women — typically 1–2% of bone mineral density per year for the first 5–10 years post-menopause. HRT (especially estradiol) is the most powerful intervention available. But the next-best lever is **resistance training plus creatine.**

 Multiple trials in postmenopausal women have shown creatine combined with resistance training produces:

- Significantly better preservation of femoral neck bone density vs. resistance training alone

- Improved lower-body strength and lean mass

- Reduced fall risk (a major driver of fracture mortality in women over 65)

 The mechanism appears to involve creatine's effects on osteoblast activity (the bone-building cells) and on the muscle contractions that signal bone to remodel. Creatine without resistance training doesn't show the same bone effect — the two are synergistic.

 For women on HRT plus a strength training program (see [HRT after 40](https://www.directcare.ai/blog/hrt-after-40-what-actually-changes/) and the [3x-week resistance workout](https://www.directcare.ai/blog/3x-week-resistance-workout-glp1-muscle-preservation/)), creatine is the missing third leg of the protocol.

### The longevity case.

 Three threads:

- Sarcopenia prevention. Loss of muscle mass is one of the strongest predictors of mortality in adults over 70. Creatine combined with any resistance training measurably slows that loss in published trials.

- Glucose metabolism. Creatine increases GLUT-4 expression in muscle, modestly improving insulin sensitivity. The metabolic effect is small but real.

- Cardiovascular health. Creatine doesn't directly affect cholesterol, but the downstream effects of preserved muscle mass and improved insulin sensitivity stack with other cardiovascular protective protocols.

### Dosing — simpler than the internet makes it.

 The protocol the published trials use, and that we recommend:

- 3–5 grams of creatine monohydrate, daily, indefinitely.

- Take with any meal. Timing relative to workouts is essentially irrelevant — the brain and muscle stores saturate over weeks, not minutes.

- Mix into water, juice, coffee, smoothies. It dissolves well in warm liquids; stir more in cold ones.

- No need to "load" at higher doses for the first week. Old protocols recommended 20g/day for 5 days; the modern data shows 3–5g/day daily reaches saturation in 3–4 weeks with fewer side effects.

 **Form:** stick with **creatine monohydrate**. The fancier forms (HCL, ethyl ester, buffered) are marketing — they don't outperform monohydrate in head-to-head trials and they cost more. Monohydrate is the form 99% of the published research used.

### Safety: what women are usually worried about.

 Three concerns women ask us about most:

 **1. Will I bulk up?** No. Creatine increases intramuscular water content slightly, which can produce a 1–3 lb scale weight increase in the first month — this isn't fat, isn't muscle bulk, and stabilizes. Women who lift heavy and eat aggressively will gain muscle on creatine because of the resistance training, not because of the creatine alone. Women who do moderate exercise will see no visible body composition change beyond slightly better-defined muscle.

 **2. Is it hard on the kidneys?** Decades of safety data say no. Multiple long-term trials (5+ years) have shown no negative kidney effects in healthy adults. Standard creatinine blood tests can read slightly elevated on creatine — that's a measurement artifact, not actual kidney impairment. If you have pre-existing kidney disease, talk to your clinician first; otherwise, creatine has one of the cleanest safety records of any supplement studied.

 **3. Will it interact with my HRT or thyroid medication?** No published interactions with HRT, thyroid medication, GLP-1s, or most common prescriptions. Creatine doesn't go through the cytochrome P450 system that mediates most drug-drug interactions.

### What's not worth the money.

- Creatine HCL, ethyl ester, buffered creatine, "micronized creatine plus..." — all marketing. Monohydrate is the gold standard, costs $15–25 for a 3-month supply, and dissolves fine.

- Pre-workout drinks containing creatine. Usually too low a dose (1–2 g per scoop) and combined with stimulants, artificial sweeteners, and proprietary blends that obscure the actual creatine content.

- Creatine gummies. Same problem — typical dose is 1–1.5 g per serving, so you'd need 3–4 gummies a day to reach a clinically meaningful dose, and the cost-per-gram is 5–10x higher than monohydrate powder.

 For most of the women we work with, the supplement stack worth taking is fairly short — covered in [the 4 supplement gaps every patient has](https://www.directcare.ai/blog/magnesium-vitamin-d-supplement-gaps/). Add creatine as a fifth, and you've covered the major evidence-backed bases.

- In perimenopause without HRT: creatine + resistance training is the strongest non-hormonal lever for preserving bone, muscle, and cognitive function.

### The bottom line.

 Creatine has been mismarketed for 25 years. The cleanest version of the truth: it's a small, cheap, exceptionally well-studied molecule that improves cognitive performance, supports bone health, preserves muscle mass, and has the safest profile of essentially any supplement on the market.

 If you're a woman over 35 — and especially over 50 — there's a strong evidence-based case for it being in your daily routine, regardless of whether you ever set foot in a gym.
