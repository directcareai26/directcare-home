## Early Signs of Perimenopause Women Often Miss in Their 40s

 Perimenopause is the transition before menopause. It often begins in the 40s, sometimes earlier, and the first signs are frequently changes in sleep, cycles and mood, not hot flashes.

 Perimenopause is the transition leading up to menopause. It often begins in the 40s, sometimes the late 30s, and commonly lasts several years. Early signs can include less restful sleep, cycles that change in length, new anxiety or irritability, and brain fog, sometimes before any hot flashes.

 Menopause is a single point in time: 12 months after the final period. Perimenopause is the stretch before it, and for many women it starts earlier and looks different than they were told to expect.

### What perimenopause is

 Perimenopause is the transition during which the ovaries' hormone production becomes less predictable. Estrogen does not simply decline in a straight line. It swings, sometimes higher than before and sometimes lower, and ovulation becomes less regular.

 Researchers stage this transition by the menstrual cycle. Under the widely used STRAW+10 criteria, the early transition begins when cycle length starts to vary by seven days or more from one cycle to the next. The late transition is marked by gaps of 60 days or more between periods.

### When it starts and how long it lasts

 Menopause happens at about age 51 on average in the United States. The transition leading up to it most often begins in the mid-40s. For some women it begins in the early 40s or late 30s.

 It commonly lasts around four years, and for some women it runs closer to eight. That is a long time to feel "off" without a name for it, which is part of why the early phase is so often missed.

### The early signs that get missed

 Hot flashes are the symptom everyone knows. They are often not the first one. Many women first notice changes like these while their periods are still fairly regular:

- Sleep that stops being restful. Waking in the early hours, lighter sleep, or waking hot.

- Cycles that shift. Shorter or longer cycles, heavier or lighter flow, or less predictable timing.

- Mood changes. New anxiety, irritability or low mood. Research shows the risk of depressive symptoms is higher during the transition.

- Brain fog. Trouble finding words, losing a train of thought, or weaker concentration.

- Body changes. A different response to the same food and the same training, often with more weight carried around the middle.

 A 2016 review in the Journal of Women's Health describes hot flashes, sleep disturbance, mood changes and vaginal dryness as the core symptoms of the transition, and notes that they vary widely from one woman to the next.

 If this sounds familiar

 These symptoms are common, they have a physiological basis, and they are not something a woman has to simply wait out. A clinician can help sort out what is going on and what the options are.

### Why a blood test often cannot settle it

 Because hormone levels swing from week to week during perimenopause, one blood test is a snapshot of a moving target. An FSH or estradiol result can be normal one month and very different the next.

 For that reason, clinicians usually identify perimenopause in women in their mid-40s and older from symptoms and cycle history, not from a single hormone result. ### What else can look like perimenopause

 Several common conditions overlap with these symptoms:

- Thyroid disorders

- Iron deficiency, with or without anemia

- Depression and anxiety disorders

- Sleep apnea

- Pregnancy, which is still possible during perimenopause

 That overlap is a reason to have symptoms assessed and not assumed. Our article on [ferritin in women](https://www.directcare.ai/blog/ferritin-iron-deficiency-women-mistaken-for-menopause) covers one of the most frequent mix-ups.

### When to talk with a clinician

 It is reasonable to raise these symptoms at any point, and more so when they affect sleep, work or relationships. Some situations call for prompt medical review:

- Very heavy bleeding, or bleeding that soaks through protection every hour or two

- Bleeding between periods or after sex

- Periods that come closer than every three weeks

- Any bleeding after 12 months without a period

- Low mood that is persistent or includes thoughts of self-harm

### What the options look like

 Care is individual. Depending on symptoms, history and preference, a clinician may discuss sleep and lifestyle measures, non-hormonal medicines, or hormone therapy.

 The 2022 position statement of The North American Menopause Society describes hormone therapy as the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. It also states that for women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause who have no contraindications, the balance of benefit and risk is generally favorable. Whether it is suitable for a particular woman depends on her medical history, and that is a decision to make with a licensed clinician.

 Tracking helps that conversation. A few weeks of notes on cycle dates, sleep and symptoms give a clinician far more to work with than memory does.
