ED as a Cardiovascular Warning Sign: the labs that confirm it

Erectile dysfunction is often the first outward sign of endothelial disease, showing up years before a cardiac event. The right lab panel can tell you whether your ED is a plumbing problem, a hormone problem, or an early warning about your arteries.

Erectile dysfunction rarely arrives alone. In men under 60, new-onset ED is one of the earliest clinical signals of endothelial dysfunction — the same process that later shows up as coronary artery disease, stroke, or peripheral vascular disease. The penile arteries are simply smaller (roughly 1–2 mm) than the coronaries (3–4 mm), so they clog and stiffen first.

That's why treating ED as a Viagra problem — without asking why the vasculature is failing — misses the point. The pill may work. The artery is still sick.

Why ED shows up before the heart attack

The evidence here is not subtle. In the landmark Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial analysis, men who developed ED had a 25% higher risk of a subsequent cardiovascular event over seven years compared to men without ED, independent of traditional risk factors (Thompson et al., JAMA 2005). A 2018 meta-analysis in Circulation found ED preceded a coronary event by an average of 3 to 5 years.

The takeaway in plain English: if you're 45 and your erections have quietly gotten less reliable over two years, your cardiologist should probably know before your urologist does.

The mechanism is shared. Both erections and coronary flow depend on nitric oxide (NO) — produced by healthy endothelial cells lining the arteries. When endothelial function declines from insulin resistance, oxidized LDL, chronic inflammation, or smoking, NO drops. The penis notices first.

What separates vascular ED from hormonal ED

Not every case of ED is cardiovascular. Roughly, the buckets are:

  • Vascular (endothelial): gradual onset, worse over months to years, morning erections diminished but not gone, often with elevated lipids or glucose.
  • Hormonal (low testosterone, elevated prolactin, thyroid): reduced libido and reduced function, fatigue, mood changes, loss of morning erections.
  • Neurogenic (diabetic neuropathy, post-surgical, pelvic nerve): more abrupt, often with numbness or bladder changes.
  • Psychogenic: situational, preserved morning erections, tied to stress or relationship context.

Most real cases are mixed. A 52-year-old with an HbA1c of 6.1, ApoB of 130, and total testosterone of 320 ng/dL has all three going at once. Which is why the labs matter.

The lab panel worth running before you treat ED

If you're going to work up ED seriously — not just paper over it — the labs worth having on the table look like this. Think of it as three layers: cardiometabolic, hormonal, and inflammatory.

Cardiometabolic layer

  • Lipid panel with ApoB. Standard LDL-C undercounts risk in men with metabolic syndrome. ApoB counts every atherogenic particle. Optimal is <90 mg/dL; <60 mg/dL if you already have vascular disease (Sniderman et al., JACC 2019).
  • HbA1c and fasting insulin. ED is roughly 3x more common in men with type 2 diabetes (Malavige & Levy, J Sex Med 2009). Fasting insulin catches insulin resistance years before HbA1c drifts.
  • Lp(a). A one-time test. Elevated Lp(a) (>50 mg/dL) is an independent risk factor for endothelial disease and often the reason a young-ish, thin man has ED with otherwise clean labs.

Hormonal layer

  • Total and free testosterone, drawn between 7–10 a.m. Total <300 ng/dL on two morning draws meets the clinical threshold for hypogonadism per AUA guidelines. But symptoms often start in the 300–400 range.
  • SHBG. Determines how much of your testosterone is actually bioavailable. High SHBG (common with age, alcohol, or thyroid issues) means a "normal" total testosterone can still leave you functionally low.
  • LH and FSH. Distinguishes primary (testicular) from secondary (pituitary/hypothalamic) hypogonadism — the treatment path is different.
  • Prolactin and TSH. Cheap, high-yield. Elevated prolactin can shut down libido and erections independent of testosterone.
  • Estradiol (sensitive assay). Both too-low and too-high estradiol impair erectile function.

Inflammatory layer

  • hs-CRP. A marker of vascular inflammation. >2.0 mg/L suggests ongoing endothelial stress.
  • Homocysteine. Elevated levels correlate with reduced NO bioavailability and worse erectile scores (Sansone et al., Andrology 2018).

{callout: The core insight} In a man under 60, new erectile dysfunction is a vascular event in slow motion — and the right lab panel usually reveals it before any imaging does.

What the pattern usually looks like

A few common signatures we see:

  • ApoB 120+, HbA1c 5.8–6.2, hs-CRP 3+, normal testosterone. This is endothelial ED. PDE5 inhibitors will work, but the real fix is lipid and glucose control — often a statin, sometimes a GLP-1 if BMI and A1c justify it, plus aggressive lifestyle change.
  • Testosterone 280, LH low-normal, SHBG low, elevated fasting insulin. This is metabolic hypogonadism. Weight loss and insulin sensitivity often move testosterone 100–150 ng/dL on their own before any hormone therapy is considered.
  • Testosterone 240, LH high, everything else clean. Primary hypogonadism. This is where TRT is a genuine consideration, with the standard workup for causes.
  • Everything normal, ED still present. Look at sleep (undiagnosed apnea is a major hidden driver), SSRIs, alcohol, and psychogenic factors.

What actually changes the arteries

PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) treat the symptom by amplifying the NO signal you still have. They do not fix the endothelium. What does, based on the strongest evidence:

  • LDL/ApoB reduction. Statins improve erectile function scores in men with vascular ED (meta-analysis, J Sex Med 2014), likely via endothelial recovery, not cholesterol per se.
  • Weight loss of 5–10% in men with metabolic syndrome improves IIEF scores meaningfully (Esposito et al., JAMA 2004).
  • Structured aerobic exercise, 160+ minutes per week, produces improvements comparable to low-dose PDE5 therapy in some trials (Gerbild et al., Sex Med 2018).
  • Correcting hypogonadism, when genuinely present, restores libido and often erectile response — but does not fix underlying vascular disease.
If ED is your body's smoke alarm, the goal isn't just to silence it. It's to find the fire.

When to escalate beyond labs

If your labs are clean and ED persists, or if you have chest pain on exertion, family history of early cardiac events, or an ankle-brachial index abnormality, ask for a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score. A CAC of 0 is genuinely reassuring. A CAC over 100 in a 50-year-old with ED changes the entire treatment conversation — the ED becomes secondary to the cardiac risk.

The plain-English plan

1. Treat new ED in men over 40 as a cardiovascular question until proven otherwise. 2. Get the labs above on the table before deciding whether the answer is a PDE5 inhibitor, hormone therapy, metabolic treatment, or all three. 3. Don't skip the lifestyle math. Endothelium responds to sleep, exercise, and glucose control on a timeline of weeks to months. 4. Revisit labs in 90–120 days after any intervention — you want to see ApoB, HbA1c, and hs-CRP moving, not just symptoms.

ED that's really a vascular story deserves a vascular workup. The pill is easy. The underlying artery deserves better.
