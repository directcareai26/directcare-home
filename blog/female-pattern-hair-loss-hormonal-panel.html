Female Hair Loss: The Hormonal Panel to Run First

Female-pattern hair loss is rarely just genetic — it's usually genetics amplified by an underlying hormonal or nutritional signal. The right first-line panel catches the drivers that actually change your treatment plan.

Female-pattern hair loss (FPHL) — the clinical term is female androgenetic alopecia — affects roughly 40% of women by age 50, according to the American Academy of Dermatology. But the label is misleading. In women, the pattern you see in the mirror (widening part, thinning crown, preserved frontal hairline) is genetic susceptibility colliding with a hormonal, thyroid, or nutritional trigger. Treat the trigger and the follicle recovers ground. Miss the trigger and topical minoxidil is doing all the work alone.

Here's the panel a good clinician runs before writing anything — and what each number is actually telling you.

Why labs come before a prescription

The whole point is to find the amplifier.

Most women who present with diffuse thinning have at least one correctable input: low ferritin, subclinical hypothyroidism, elevated androgens from PCOS, postpartum shifts, or a recent crash diet. A 2022 review in Skin Appendage Disorders found that up to 30% of women evaluated for FPHL had a coexisting endocrine or nutritional abnormality that materially changed management.

If you skip the workup, you're guessing. The labs worth having before a serious protocol are the ones that either change the diagnosis (is this FPHL or telogen effluvium?) or change the treatment (do we add spironolactone? treat thyroid? repair iron stores?).

The first-line hormonal and nutritional panel

In plain English, these are the seven that matter.

A thorough workup would typically include:

  • Ferritin — the iron storage marker. Target >40–70 ng/mL for hair; below 30 is a red flag even if hemoglobin is normal.
  • TSH, Free T4, Free T3 — thyroid dysfunction (both hypo and hyper) is a classic driver of diffuse shedding.
  • Total and free testosterone — screens for androgen excess.
  • DHEA-S — adrenal androgen output; elevated in PCOS and adrenal hyperplasia.
  • SHBG — sex hormone binding globulin. Low SHBG means more free androgen, even when total testosterone looks normal.
  • Vitamin D (25-OH) — deficiency is associated with FPHL severity in multiple case-control studies.
  • Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel — rules out anemia, kidney, and liver issues that mimic or worsen shedding.

Depending on the history, a clinician may add prolactin, fasting insulin, HbA1c, zinc, or a full androgen panel including androstenedione and 17-OH progesterone.

{callout: The one takeaway} Female-pattern hair loss is a pattern, not a diagnosis — the hormonal panel tells you which underlying driver to actually treat.

What each result actually changes

Tuned to your numbers, not a generic protocol.

Ferritin under 40

Iron is a cofactor for the enzymes that keep hair in the growth (anagen) phase. A 2013 study in the Journal of Korean Medical Science found significantly lower ferritin in women with FPHL vs. controls. Oral iron with vitamin C, rechecked at 8–12 weeks, is standard. Don't supplement blindly — iron overload has its own risks.

TSH out of range

Even subclinical hypothyroidism (TSH 4.5–10 with normal Free T4) can drive telogen effluvium. Treating the thyroid often resolves the shedding within 3–6 months without any hair-specific intervention.

Elevated testosterone, DHEA-S, or low SHBG

This is the PCOS/hyperandrogenism bucket. Here, anti-androgen therapy — most commonly spironolactone within the published 50–200 mg/day range — becomes a rational addition to minoxidil. Oral contraceptives with low-androgenic progestins are another lever. The point: without the androgen numbers, you can't justify the anti-androgen.

Vitamin D under 30 ng/mL

Repletion is cheap, safe, and correlated with improved hair density in small trials. Not a standalone treatment, but a correctable input.

Everything normal

This is genuinely useful information. It points toward primary androgenetic alopecia driven by follicular sensitivity to normal androgen levels, and the treatment shifts to topical minoxidil, low-level laser therapy, oral minoxidil at low doses, and — where indicated — spironolactone.

FPHL vs. telogen effluvium — the panel helps here too

What actually changes when you know which one it is.

Telogen effluvium (TE) is diffuse shedding triggered by a stressor: childbirth, illness, surgery, rapid weight loss, a new medication, severe caloric restriction. It usually shows up 2–4 months after the trigger and resolves within 6–9 months once the trigger is removed.

FPHL is a chronic miniaturization of follicles that doesn't self-resolve.

The two overlap constantly — many women have FPHL unmasked by a TE episode. The lab panel matters because:

  • A crashed ferritin or a new thyroid issue points to a TE component that will resolve with correction.
  • Normal labs plus classic pattern points to FPHL that needs ongoing treatment.
  • Both can be true, and both can be treated in parallel.
If your part is widening, your ponytail is thinner, and your shower drain has been busy for more than six months — the answer isn't a new shampoo. It's a blood draw.

Timing and interpretation notes

The small details that make the panel actually useful.

  • Cycle timing for androgens: in premenopausal women, draw testosterone and DHEA-S in the early follicular phase (days 2–5) when possible for cleanest interpretation.
  • Hold biotin for 72 hours before the draw. High-dose biotin (common in hair supplements) interferes with TSH and other immunoassays and can produce falsely reassuring or falsely alarming results.
  • Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant — it rises with inflammation. If CRP is elevated, a "normal" ferritin may still reflect true iron deficiency. Interpret in context.
  • Free testosterone by equilibrium dialysis or calculated from total + SHBG is more reliable than direct free T immunoassays.

What treatment looks like once the panel is back

The plan gets specific, not generic.

A typical evidence-based stack, layered based on what the labs showed:

1. Correct the deficiency or endocrine driver — iron, thyroid, vitamin D, PCOS management. 2. Topical minoxidil 5% once daily, or 2% twice daily — the best-studied first-line, per AAD guidelines. 3. Low-dose oral minoxidil — increasingly used off-label; published protocols typically start at 0.625–1.25 mg daily and titrate. 4. Spironolactone — for confirmed androgen excess or FPHL not responding to topical treatment; published range 50–200 mg/day. 5. Adjuncts — low-level laser therapy devices, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), and nutritional support.

A clinician reviewing your symptoms, history, and labs (when you've run them) can sequence these in a way that matches your driver — not a generic recipe.

The bottom line

Female-pattern hair loss deserves a workup, not a guess. The first-line panel — ferritin, full thyroid, androgens, SHBG, vitamin D, CBC/CMP — takes one blood draw and reshapes the treatment plan for roughly a third of women who run it. If you're already six months into a topical and nothing is moving, the missing piece is almost always on that panel.

Real labs, plain-English plan. That's the standard of care worth asking for.
