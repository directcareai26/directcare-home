Ferritin in Women: The Deficiency Mistaken for Menopause

Low ferritin causes fatigue, hair shedding, brain fog, and poor sleep — the exact symptoms women get told are perimenopause. Here's how to tell them apart with one $15 lab.

A 43-year-old walks into her primary care appointment exhausted. Her hair is thinning at the part. She can't finish a workout she used to crush. She's cold when everyone else is fine. Her PCP nods, says "sounds like perimenopause," and hands her a pamphlet.

Maybe it is perimenopause. But before anyone touches her hormones, someone should look at her ferritin. In menstruating women, iron deficiency is roughly three times more common than in men, and the symptom overlap with perimenopause is nearly total (Camaschella, NEJM 2015).

This is the lab that gets missed the most, and the one that changes the most when you fix it.

Why ferritin, not just hemoglobin — the number that actually moves first

Most doctors screen for anemia with a CBC. Hemoglobin looks normal, they move on. The problem: hemoglobin is the last domino to fall.

Ferritin — your stored iron — drops months or years before your red blood cell count does. You can have iron deficiency without anemia (IDWA), feel awful, and pass every standard screen (Soppi, Journal of Blood Medicine 2018).

Think of ferritin as your iron savings account and hemoglobin as your checking account. You can be broke on savings for a long time before checks start bouncing.

  • Hemoglobin: the oxygen-carrier in circulation right now
  • Serum iron: iron in transit, fluctuates hour to hour
  • Transferrin saturation: how full the transport trucks are
  • Ferritin: the storage tank — the one that tells you the truth about reserves

If you only run one, run ferritin.

The symptom overlap that fools everyone — in plain English

Here's why iron deficiency gets misfiled as perimenopause. Compare the lists:

Low ferritin symptoms: fatigue, hair shedding (especially at the crown and part), brittle nails, cold hands and feet, exercise intolerance, restless legs at night, brain fog, low mood, shortness of breath on stairs, tinnitus, ice cravings (pagophagia).

Perimenopause symptoms: fatigue, hair thinning, brain fog, low mood, sleep disruption, temperature dysregulation, exercise intolerance.

The overlap is not subtle. And here's the kicker: perimenopause makes iron deficiency worse. Erratic cycles in the mid-40s often mean heavier, longer, or more frequent bleeds — which drains iron faster than the body can replace it from diet alone (Fraser et al., Fertility and Sterility 2015).

So it's not either/or. It's often both. But treating the hormones without fixing the iron leaves half the symptoms in place.

What a "normal" ferritin actually means — the lab range trap

Most labs flag ferritin as low only below 15 ng/mL. This is where women get told their labs are "fine" while they feel terrible.

The research disagrees with the lab range:

  • Symptoms of iron deficiency commonly appear below 30 ng/mL (WHO threshold for depleted stores)
  • Hair loss studies suggest a floor around 40–70 ng/mL for regrowth (Trost et al., JAAD 2006)
  • Restless legs syndrome improves when ferritin is pushed above 75–100 ng/mL (Allen et al., Sleep Medicine 2018)
  • Optimal range for symptomatic women: roughly 50–150 ng/mL
A ferritin of 22 is technically "normal" and clinically miserable.

One caveat: ferritin is an acute-phase reactant. Infection, inflammation, obesity, or liver disease can inflate it and mask a true deficiency. That's why a smart workup pairs ferritin with CRP and, if the picture is muddy, transferrin saturation.

{callout: The one takeaway} If you're a menstruating woman with fatigue, hair shedding, or brain fog, don't accept "your labs are normal" without seeing your actual ferritin number — and don't accept anything under 50 ng/mL as adequate if you're symptomatic.

The labs worth running before you blame hormones — tuned to your numbers

Before starting HRT, before starting minoxidil, before assuming this is "just aging," the panel worth having in hand:

  • Ferritin — storage iron
  • CBC — hemoglobin, MCV (low MCV suggests iron deficiency; high MCV suggests B12/folate)
  • Serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation — the transport picture
  • CRP — to interpret ferritin honestly
  • TSH, free T4 — thyroid mimics all of this too
  • Vitamin D, B12 — the other common fatigue drivers
  • Estradiol, FSH — if perimenopause is genuinely on the table (understanding these fluctuate cycle-to-cycle in the 40s)

Fixing low ferritin: what actually works — and how long it takes

Repletion is not fast. Plan on months, not weeks.

Oral iron is first-line for most women. The current evidence favors:

  • Every-other-day dosing rather than daily — improves absorption by reducing the hepcidin spike that blocks the next dose (Stoffel et al., Lancet Haematology 2017)
  • Standard elemental iron doses range from 40–100 mg on dosing days; work with a clinician on your specific number
  • Take with vitamin C (or an orange), on an empty stomach when tolerated
  • Avoid coffee, tea, calcium, and dairy within two hours
  • Expect GI side effects; different forms (bisglycinate, sulfate, gluconate) tolerate differently

Recheck ferritin at 8–12 weeks. Hemoglobin corrects in weeks; ferritin stores take 3–6 months to fully rebuild. If ferritin isn't rising, the two usual suspects are ongoing heavy bleeding or malabsorption (celiac, H. pylori, PPIs).

IV iron is worth discussing with your clinician if you can't tolerate oral, if oral isn't working, or if bleeding is severe and you need to catch up fast.

Address the source. Iron in equals iron out. If cycles are heavy — soaking a pad or tampon in under two hours, clots larger than a quarter, cycles longer than 7 days — that's a gynecology conversation, not just an iron conversation.

When it's iron and perimenopause — the honest answer

Often both are true. Fixing ferritin first is the cleaner experiment: iron repletion has a well-defined timeline (8–12 weeks to feel different, 3–6 months for stores), and once it's done, you can see what symptoms remain.

What's left after ferritin is above 50–70 ng/mL is a much clearer signal of what hormones might actually be doing. That's when the HRT conversation becomes useful instead of a shot in the dark.

The wrong order — starting hormones while iron-deficient — leaves you guessing why you still feel tired, still shedding hair, still foggy. The right order saves months.

The bottom line

Ferritin is the cheapest, highest-yield lab in women's health, and the one most likely to be under-ordered or misinterpreted. If you're a menstruating woman over 35 with fatigue, hair loss, or brain fog, the number to know is your ferritin — and "normal" on the lab report isn't the same as optimal for how you feel.
