## GLP-1 Maintenance Dosing: staying off without rebound

 After you hit your GLP-1 goal weight, the question isn't whether to stop — it's how to taper, maintain, or cycle without regaining. Here's what the trial data and clinical practice actually suggest.

 You lost the weight. The jeans fit. Now the harder question: what happens when you stop the injection?

 This is the part of the GLP-1 conversation that most patients only encounter once they're already at goal — and it's the part with the least marketing gloss and the most published evidence. The short version: stopping cold usually leads to significant regain, but staying on the full weight-loss dose forever isn't the only alternative. There's a middle path, and it's what most experienced clinicians are quietly doing with their patients.

### What the rebound data actually shows in plain English

 The STEP 1 extension trial followed patients who had lost roughly 17% of body weight on semaglutide 2.4 mg. One year after stopping the drug (and the lifestyle program), participants had regained about two-thirds of their lost weight, and most cardiometabolic improvements — blood pressure, lipids, A1c — reverted toward baseline (Wilding et al., Diabetes Obesity and Metabolism, 2022).

 SURMOUNT-4 did something similar with tirzepatide. Patients who reached goal on tirzepatide and were then switched to placebo regained about 14% of body weight over 52 weeks. The group that stayed on tirzepatide continued to lose a small additional amount (Aronne et al., JAMA, 2024).

 The pattern is consistent across the class: **appetite regulation returns to your pre-medication set point within weeks of the last dose.** Ghrelin climbs. Satiety signaling drops. The food noise that quieted comes back.

 This isn't a failure of willpower. It's pharmacology. GLP-1 receptor agonists work while they're in your system; when they clear, the biology they were compensating for is still there.

### Why a maintenance dose isn't the same as a weight-loss dose

 The titration schedules on the label — 0.25 → 0.5 → 1.0 → 1.7 → 2.4 mg for semaglutide, or 2.5 → 5 → 7.5 → 10 → 12.5 → 15 mg for tirzepatide — are built for the loss phase. They escalate appetite suppression to drive a caloric deficit.

 Maintenance is a different physiological job. You're not trying to create a deficit anymore; you're trying to hold a new set point. Many patients find that a lower dose, a longer interval between injections, or both, is enough to prevent regain without the appetite suppression feeling as heavy.

 Common maintenance strategies clinicians use (all within published titration ranges):

- Dose reduction: dropping back one or two titration steps and holding there.

- Interval extension: moving from weekly to every 10–14 days, monitoring weight weekly.

- Seasonal cycling: full dose during high-risk periods (holidays, travel, high-stress work cycles), lower dose otherwise.

 None of these are one-size-fits-all. The right answer depends on how much weight you lost, how fast, your lean mass, your hunger patterns off the drug, and whether you have underlying metabolic disease.

 {callout: The key takeaway} Rebound after GLP-1s isn't a character flaw — it's the predictable return of pre-treatment biology. Maintenance dosing exists precisely because "just stop" rarely works.

### How to tell if you're ready to taper without guessing

 There's no lab that says "you're done." But there are signals worth tracking before you drop a dose:

 1. **Weight stability at goal for 8–12 weeks.** Not still trending down. Stable. 2. **Established eating patterns.** Consistent protein intake (roughly 1.2–1.6 g/kg goal body weight), regular meals, minimal binge episodes. 3. **Resistance training in place.** GLP-1 weight loss includes lean mass loss — often 25–40% of total loss is fat-free mass in sedentary patients (Wilding et al., 2021). You want that muscle back before you taper. 4. **Sleep and stress in a manageable range.** Cortisol-driven hunger is real and will exploit any appetite gap.

 The labs worth having before a serious taper attempt — if you choose to run them — typically include a fasting metabolic panel, A1c, fasting insulin, a full lipid panel, and thyroid markers. These give you a baseline to compare against if weight or symptoms drift after dose changes.

### What a real maintenance protocol looks like

 Here's the frame most patients land in, though the specifics vary:

#### The step-down

 Drop one titration level. Hold for 4–6 weeks. Weigh yourself the same morning each week under the same conditions. If weight is stable (± 2 lbs), consider dropping again. If it trends up two weeks in a row, go back to the prior step.

#### The interval stretch

 Instead of lowering the dose, keep the dose and extend the interval — from every 7 days to every 9, then 10, then 14. This is often better tolerated because the peak appetite suppression stays intact; you just spend more time between peaks.

#### The off-ramp

 Some patients want fully off. That's reasonable, and it's more likely to succeed if you've spent 3–6 months at a reduced dose first, built the muscle back, and locked in the eating patterns. Even then, expect to regain 3–7% and plan for a re-initiation option if it drifts higher.

 The patients who hold their loss best are usually the ones who stopped thinking about the drug as a diet and started thinking about it as a thermostat.

### The lean mass problem nobody warned you about

 This deserves its own section because it's the single biggest predictor of rebound.

 When you lose weight fast — on any intervention — you lose muscle along with fat. GLP-1s are not uniquely bad at this, but the speed of loss (often 15–20% body weight in a year) means the absolute lean mass loss can be substantial. Less muscle means a lower resting metabolic rate, which means the caloric ceiling for maintenance is lower than it was before you started.

 If you finish a GLP-1 course 20 pounds lighter but 8 of those pounds were muscle, your maintenance calories might be 200–300 kcal/day lower than a same-weight person who built to that weight through training. That gap is where rebound lives.

 The fix isn't complicated, but it's non-negotiable:

- Resistance training 2–3x weekly , progressive overload, compound lifts. ACSM guidelines are a reasonable floor.

- Protein intake at the upper end of the range above, spread across 3–4 meals.

- Don't rush the taper. Muscle takes months to build; the drug isn't going anywhere.

### When to talk to a clinician about changing your dose

 Some situations genuinely warrant a conversation before you adjust anything:

- You've lost more than 20% of body weight and want to stop.

- You have type 2 diabetes and the GLP-1 is doing double duty on glycemic control.

- You're experiencing GI side effects that are making you want to quit abruptly.

- You've regained more than 5% after a taper attempt.

- You're planning pregnancy (GLP-1s should be discontinued well in advance).

 A thorough workup at this stage would typically include a review of your weight trajectory, current symptoms, training and nutrition data, and — if you choose to run them — updated metabolic labs. The goal is to match the dose to where your biology actually is now, not where it was when you started.

### The honest bottom line

 GLP-1s are not a course of antibiotics. There's no defined endpoint where the job is done and you walk away unchanged. For most patients with obesity or significant metabolic dysfunction, these medications are best understood as long-term tools used at varying intensities — sometimes at full weight-loss dose, sometimes at maintenance, occasionally off entirely, then back on if needed.

 That framing isn't a failure. It's how we treat almost every other chronic metabolic condition. The patients who do best are the ones who stop looking for the exit and start looking for the right long-term dose.

 If you're at or near goal and thinking about what comes next, the useful next step is a structured plan — dose, training, protein, and a check-in cadence — rather than a guess. That's the conversation worth having before you skip your next injection.
