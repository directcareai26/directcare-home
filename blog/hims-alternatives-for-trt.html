Why this matters

Low energy, reduced sex drive, and erectile difficulties do not point to one diagnosis. Sleep problems, medication effects, depression, thyroid disease, and other conditions can produce overlapping symptoms. Choosing a testosterone provider before understanding the cause puts the treatment ahead of the diagnosis.

For your 2026 decision, compare the clinical pathway rather than the ease of signing up. You need someone who can interpret symptoms alongside properly timed blood tests, discuss fertility, and explain what happens if treatment does not help. A prescription is only one part of that relationship.

This guide compares care routes, not just websites. It is educational information, not a diagnosis or a recommendation to start, stop, or change hormone treatment.

2. Endocrinologist: best for complex hormone questions

An endocrinologist specializes in hormone-related conditions. This route fits when low testosterone is accompanied by other abnormal hormone results, when the cause is unclear, or when symptoms and test results do not line up.

The goal is not simply to confirm that testosterone is low. It is to establish why it is low and whether another condition needs attention before testosterone treatment is considered.

Best for: You need a cause-focused assessment rather than a straightforward discussion about replacing testosterone.

Where an endocrinologist shines

  • Hormone interpretation: Testosterone can be assessed alongside signals from the pituitary and other endocrine systems.
  • Diagnostic complexity: A specialist can investigate findings that do not fit a simple pattern.
  • Treatment selection: The assessment can address whether TRT is appropriate or whether the underlying condition calls for another approach.

Where this route falls short

  • A focused specialist consultation does not replace general preventive care.
  • Endocrine expertise is not the same as dedicated male fertility expertise; reproductive goals can require additional input.
  • You still need clear agreement about which clinician manages prescriptions, routine monitoring, and other health conditions.

Ask whether the consultation is intended to establish a diagnosis, manage long-term treatment, or both. If several clinicians are involved, identify the person responsible for reviewing each result. Shared care needs explicit responsibilities.

Verdict: Hold TRT initiation until unexplained hormone findings have been assessed.

3. Urologist: best for fertility and reproductive concerns

A urologist is a relevant alternative when low testosterone overlaps with erectile problems, testicular concerns, urinary symptoms, or fertility goals. For fertility-specific care, look for a urologist with male reproductive expertise.

Best for: You want testosterone decisions considered alongside sperm production and reproductive health.

Where a urologist shines

  • Can assess reproductive and urinary concerns together.
  • Can examine testicular findings that cannot be evaluated fully through a questionnaire.
  • Can discuss why testosterone replacement and fertility treatment are not interchangeable.

Where this route falls short

  • Not every urology practice focuses on male infertility or hormone management.
  • A reproductive assessment does not replace investigation of broader endocrine disease when indicated.

Tell the clinician about future pregnancy plans before discussing prescriptions. External testosterone can suppress sperm production, so a desire to conceive changes the treatment conversation. Do not assume that stopping treatment later will restore fertility on your preferred schedule.

Verdict: Hold testosterone treatment until fertility goals are explicitly addressed.

What a sound TRT evaluation should establish

A good evaluation answers a sequence of clinical questions. It should not jump from feeling tired to choosing a testosterone formulation.

Symptoms

Describe the symptoms that prompted testing, when they began, and what else changed around that time. A laboratory result without relevant symptoms does not establish the same clinical picture as persistent symptoms with repeatedly low results.

Repeat testing

The American Urological Association's testosterone-deficiency guideline calls for 2 separate early-morning total testosterone tests when establishing the diagnosis. Its diagnostic framework uses 300 ng/dL as a reasonable cutoff supporting low testosterone, alongside symptoms and signs.

That threshold is not a self-prescribing rule. Your clinician must account for the testing context and decide whether additional measurements, such as free testosterone, are needed. Illness and disrupted sleep can also affect interpretation.

Cause assessment

Ask whether the results suggest a problem with testosterone production or with the hormonal signals that regulate it. Depending on your history and results, clinicians can use additional hormone tests to investigate the cause. Not every patient needs the same panel.

Fertility planning

State whether pregnancy is a current or future goal before treatment is selected. Do not treat fertility as a side-effect discussion to revisit after starting testosterone. It belongs in the initial decision.

Monitoring

Agree on what will be checked, when results will be reviewed, and who will contact you about a concerning finding. Monitoring should address both whether symptoms improve and whether treatment creates a safety problem.

Blood counts are part of this safety discussion because testosterone can increase red blood cell production. The Endocrine Society's testosterone-treatment guideline identifies hematocrit above 54% as a reason for clinician-directed interruption and evaluation. Do not change your dose yourself in response to a result.

A normal-looking testosterone number is not the only treatment goal. Ask how your clinician will judge symptom benefit, adverse effects, and whether continuing treatment remains appropriate.

A useful reason to switch in 2026 is a mismatch between the care you need and the pathway you have—not an assumption that another brand automatically provides better medicine. These are clinical reasons to consider a different route, not claims about failures at Hims.

  • You need a cause explained. An unclear hormone pattern calls for diagnostic work, not simply another prescription request.
  • You want to conceive. A reproductive specialist can address sperm production before testosterone replacement is considered.
  • You need an examination. Testicular changes or other physical findings require appropriate in-person assessment.
  • Your symptoms persist. Ongoing fatigue or sexual symptoms deserve reassessment rather than automatic dose escalation.
  • Your care needs coordination. Several prescribers should know about each other's medications and test results.

Before changing providers, request your laboratory reports and medication history. Share the formulation, dose, and timing of your last treatment with the next clinician. Testing while taking testosterone answers different questions from testing before treatment, so the new clinician needs that context.

Do not interrupt prescribed treatment solely to produce a different laboratory result. Arrange the transition with a clinician and clarify who remains responsible during the handover.

FAQ

What's the best Hims TRT alternative in 2026?

DirectCare AI is a relevant alternative if you want a telehealth TRT evaluation with a licensed clinician. An endocrinologist fits complex hormone questions, while a urologist with reproductive expertise fits fertility concerns.

Is Hims the same as a testosterone clinic?

An online men's health platform is not automatically equivalent to a testosterone-focused clinical service. Confirm Hims' current scope and ask what diagnostic testing, prescribing, monitoring, and referral arrangements apply to your situation.

How many testosterone tests do I need before TRT?

The American Urological Association recommends 2 separate early-morning total testosterone measurements when establishing testosterone deficiency. Your clinician also needs compatible symptoms and may order additional tests to interpret the results.

Does testosterone below 300 ng/dL mean I need TRT?

No, a result below 300 ng/dL does not automatically mean you need TRT. The American Urological Association uses that threshold to support diagnosis alongside symptoms and repeat testing; treatment requires an individual clinical assessment.

Can TRT affect my ability to have children?

Yes, external testosterone can suppress sperm production. Tell your clinician about current and future fertility goals before starting treatment, and seek reproductive expertise when needed.

Should I choose an endocrinologist or a urologist?

Choose an endocrinologist for complex hormonal causes and a urologist with male reproductive expertise for fertility-focused concerns. Some patients need both, particularly when abnormal hormone findings overlap with reproductive goals.

Can I switch testosterone providers without stopping treatment?

Plan the transition with your current and next clinician rather than stopping or continuing on your own. Share your treatment details and laboratory reports so responsibility for prescribing and monitoring stays clear.

One last thing

Before comparing providers, write down your fertility plans—even if having children feels distant. Testosterone replacement can improve appropriately selected patients' symptoms while suppressing sperm production. Those outcomes can happen together.

For your 2026 decision, bring three things to the consultation: your symptoms, your complete medication list, and your reproductive goals. A useful plan explains what the results mean in plain English, why treatment is or is not appropriate, and what happens next.
