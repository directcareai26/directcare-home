## Hydration and Aging: What the NIH Serum Sodium Study Found

 A 25-year NIH analysis linked blood sodium at the high end of normal in middle age, a sign of lower fluid intake, with faster biological aging and more chronic disease.

 In an NIH analysis of 15,752 adults followed for 25 years, those with middle-age serum sodium above 142 mmol/L had a 39% higher risk of developing chronic diseases and up to 50% higher odds of being biologically older than their age. The study shows an association and does not prove that drinking more water slows aging.

 Advice to drink more water is everywhere, and most of it comes without evidence. In 2023, researchers at the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute published one of the larger attempts to test whether hydration habits relate to how people age.

#### What the study did

 The team used data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study: 15,752 adults who enrolled between ages 45 and 66 and were followed for 25 years.

 They could not measure how much each person drank. They used serum sodium, the sodium level on a routine blood panel, as a stand-in. When a person takes in less fluid, sodium in the blood moves toward the upper end of the normal range, which is about 135 to 146 mmol/L.

 They also estimated each person's biological age from a set of routine health markers, including blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar.

#### What it found

- Adults with sodium above 142 mmol/L had a 39% higher risk of developing chronic diseases such as heart failure, stroke, diabetes and dementia

- They had up to 50% higher odds of being biologically older than their actual age

- Sodium above 144 mmol/L was linked with a 21% higher risk of dying early

 All of these values sit inside the normal range. A lab report would not flag them.

 Association, not proof

 The study did not test what happens when people drink more. The authors wrote that trials are needed to confirm the link. Other things can raise sodium, and people who drink more water may differ in other ways.

#### What you can take from it

- Serum sodium is already on the basic metabolic panel. If yours sits at 143 or above on repeat tests, ask your clinician whether fluid intake could be part of the reason

- The National Academies suggest total daily fluid of about 2.7 liters for women and 3.7 liters for men, counting all drinks and the water in food

- Pale yellow urine through the day is a simple check

#### Who should not simply drink more

 People with heart failure or kidney disease, and people taking certain medications, are sometimes told to limit fluids. Drinking very large amounts in a short time can also be dangerous. Ask a licensed clinician what is right for you.
