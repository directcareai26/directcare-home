## Lifting Through Perimenopause: rep ranges and recovery rules that hold up

 Perimenopause changes how you recover, not whether you should lift heavy. The training that holds up prioritizes lower-rep strength work, protein timing, and longer between-session recovery than you needed at 35.

 If you've been lifting for years and suddenly the same program leaves you flat, sore for four days, and stalled on the bar — you're not imagining it. Perimenopause shifts the physiology underneath your training, and the fix isn't to train less. It's to train heavier in shorter doses, eat more protein, and stop pretending you recover like you did at 32.

 Here's the program logic that holds up in the literature, and what to actually put on the whiteboard Monday morning.

### Why perimenopause changes the math

 The hormones drive the recovery curve.

 Estrogen isn't just a reproductive hormone — it's a muscle-protective one. It supports satellite cell activity, blunts inflammation, and helps maintain tendon stiffness. As estrogen fluctuates and eventually declines through the perimenopausal window (typically ages 40–55), three things get measurably harder: building muscle, recovering between sessions, and maintaining bone density.

 A 2023 review in Sports Medicine summarized it plainly: women in perimenopause show blunted muscle protein synthesis in response to the same protein dose and the same training stimulus that worked in their 30s. Translation — the input has to go up for the output to hold steady.

 The good news: resistance training is one of the few interventions that directly addresses all three problems. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommends resistance training 2–3 days per week for adults, with an emphasis on progressive overload for women in midlife specifically because of bone and sarcopenia risk.

### What rep ranges actually work now

 Heavier than you think, more often than you think.

 The old advice — "lighter weights, higher reps, more cardio for women over 40" — has aged badly. The current signal from the literature points the other direction.

 Here's the framework that holds up:

- Heavy strength work (3–6 reps): 40–50% of your weekly volume. This is where bone density, tendon stiffness, and neural drive live. Squat, deadlift, hip thrust, overhead press, weighted row, weighted carry variations.

- Hypertrophy work (6–12 reps): 40% of your weekly volume. This is where you actually add muscle tissue. Split squats, Romanian deadlifts, chest press, pull-downs, single-arm rows.

- Metabolic / accessory work (12–20 reps): 10–20% of your weekly volume. Glute medius, core, rotator cuff, calves. Not the main event.

 Note what's missing: the endless 15-rep circuits. They're not harmful, they're just not the highest-yield use of your recovery budget.

 If it doesn't force you to brace, breathe, and set up carefully, it's probably not heavy enough to move the bone density needle.

### A weekly split that respects recovery

 Three quality sessions beat five mediocre ones.

 Most women in perimenopause do better on 3 lifting days per week than 4–5, especially if sleep is already compromised. Here's a template:

 **Day 1 — Lower body, strength bias**

- Back squat or trap bar deadlift: 4 sets × 4–5 reps, 3 min rest

- Romanian deadlift: 3 sets × 6–8 reps, 2 min rest

- Split squat: 3 sets × 8 reps/side, 90 sec rest

- Weighted plank: 3 sets × 30–45 sec

 **Day 2 — Upper body, mixed**

- Overhead press: 4 sets × 5 reps, 2–3 min rest

- Weighted row (chest-supported or bent-over): 4 sets × 6–8 reps

- Push-up variation or dumbbell bench: 3 sets × 8–10 reps

- Face pulls: 3 sets × 12–15 reps

 **Day 3 — Full body, hypertrophy bias**

- Hip thrust: 4 sets × 6–8 reps

- Goblet squat or leg press: 3 sets × 8–10 reps

- Single-arm row: 3 sets × 8/side

- Farmer's carry: 3 sets × 40 meters heavy

 Between lifting days: walk daily, one optional Zone 2 cardio session (30–45 min), and one session of something you enjoy — yoga, pickleball, hiking. Skip the fasted HIIT phase; it's not doing what you were told it would.

 {callout: The one rule} If you can't recover well enough to add 2.5–5 lb to a main lift every 2–3 weeks, you're doing too much volume, not too little.

### Progression rules when the linear gains stop

 Progress still happens — it just gets quieter.

 Double progression is your friend. Pick a rep range (say, 5–7). Hit the top of the range on all sets before you add weight. When you can do 4×7 with a given load, add 2.5–5 lb next session and drop back to 5s. Climb again.

 When a lift stalls for three weeks:

 1. Deload for one week — same lifts, 60% of working weight, half the volume. 2. Check sleep (under 7 hours consistently will stall you before your program will). 3. Check protein (see below). 4. Consider whether cycle-phase symptoms are clustering with the stall — track it before you overhaul the program.

 Some women find their strength dips predictably in the late luteal phase or during heavy bleeding episodes. That's real. It's not a reason to stop lifting — it's a reason to autoregulate, drop a set, and come back the next session.

### Protein, sleep, and the recovery floor

 The training program won't outrun the recovery inputs.

 Three levers matter more than any program tweak:

 **Protein.** Aim for 1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight per day, distributed across 3–4 meals of 30–40 g each. The blunted muscle protein synthesis response in perimenopause means the per-meal dose needs to be at the higher end. A 150-lb woman is looking at roughly 110–150 g protein per day, not 60.

 **Sleep.** Under 7 hours consistently drops strength output, raises perceived exertion, and impairs glucose handling — all of which look like "the program isn't working" but are actually "you're under-recovered." If hot flashes or night sweats are wrecking sleep, that's a symptom worth talking to a clinician about, not powering through.

 **Between-session recovery.** 48 hours between hitting the same muscle group hard is the old rule. In perimenopause, 72 hours is often more honest. This is why 3 days a week frequently beats 5.

### When to consider labs and a clinical conversation

 Numbers make the plan less of a guess.

 You don't need bloodwork to start lifting well. But if you've been training consistently, eating enough protein, sleeping, and still feel like your engine is broken — labs help. The markers worth having on the table before assuming it's "just perimenopause":

- Ferritin and full iron panel — low iron is common and quietly tanks training capacity.

- TSH, free T4, free T3 — thyroid shifts often overlap perimenopause and mimic it.

- Vitamin D, B12 — cheap to check, common to be low, both affect strength and recovery.

- Fasting glucose and HbA1c — insulin sensitivity often drifts in this window.

- Estradiol, FSH, progesterone — interpreted in context of cycle timing and symptoms, not in isolation.

 If you want a clinician to look at your symptoms alongside labs and build a plan around both, that's exactly the kind of workup that turns "something's off" into an actual protocol. Whether the answer is training changes, HRT, or something simpler like fixing ferritin — a real plan beats a guess.

### The bottom line

 Lift heavy 3 days a week. Keep most of your work in the 3–12 rep range. Eat 30–40 g of protein per meal. Sleep. Recover 72 hours between hard sessions for the same muscle group. Track progress in months, not weeks.

 Perimenopause doesn't take strength training away from you. It just raises the standard for how well you have to execute the basics.
