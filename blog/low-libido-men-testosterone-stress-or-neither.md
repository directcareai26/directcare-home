## Low Libido in Men: Testosterone, Stress, or Neither?

 Low libido in men is usually one of three things: low testosterone, chronic stress and poor sleep, or a third bucket most clinics miss — medications, relationship dynamics, and metabolic disease. This guide walks through how to tell them apart before you decide on a protocol.

 Most men who walk into a clinic asking about libido already have a theory: it's my testosterone. Sometimes they're right. Often they're not — and the wrong theory leads to the wrong protocol.

 Libido is a downstream signal. It reflects hormones, yes, but also sleep architecture, dopamine tone, vascular health, relationship context, and whatever medications you've been on for the last six months. Sorting which lever actually moved is the entire job.

### What "low libido" actually means clinically — in plain English

 Low libido (clinically: hypoactive sexual desire) is a persistent drop in interest in sex that bothers you. The "bothers you" part matters — desire varies enormously between men, and there's no objectively correct frequency.

 What we look for is a change from your baseline that's lasted more than a few weeks and isn't explained by an obvious life event. That change is the symptom. The cause is what we're hunting.

 Three buckets cover roughly 90% of cases:

- Hormonal — low testosterone, high prolactin, thyroid dysfunction, or estradiol that's drifted out of range.

- Neurochemical and lifestyle — chronic stress, poor sleep, alcohol, overtraining, depression.

- Everything else — medications (especially SSRIs and finasteride), metabolic disease, relationship friction, or pelvic/vascular issues that present as low desire because sex stopped working well.

### When it's testosterone — and when it only looks like it

 Low testosterone is the diagnosis men expect, partly because it's been marketed hard and partly because the symptoms overlap with almost everything else: fatigue, low drive, brain fog, soft erections, mood flatness.

 The Endocrine Society defines clinical hypogonadism as a total testosterone consistently below roughly 264 ng/dL on two morning draws, plus symptoms (Bhasin et al., 2018). The two-draw part trips people up. A single low reading at 4 p.m. after a bad night's sleep is not a diagnosis.

 The labs worth having before a serious testosterone conversation typically include:

- Total and free testosterone (morning, fasted, ideally two separate draws)

- SHBG — because high SHBG can make total T look fine while free T is low

- LH and FSH — to distinguish primary (testicular) from secondary (pituitary) causes

- Prolactin — elevated prolactin crushes libido independently of testosterone and can flag a pituitary issue

- Estradiol (sensitive assay) , TSH , fasting glucose and A1c

 If your testosterone is 450 ng/dL and your libido is gone, testosterone is probably not the answer — and adding more is unlikely to fix the real problem.
 Men with mid-range testosterone and low libido often have a prolactin issue, a thyroid issue, an SSRI on board, or a sleep problem masquerading as a hormone problem.

### When it's stress, sleep, and dopamine — the most common miss

 Desire runs on dopamine. Chronic stress, under-sleeping, and constant low-grade overstimulation blunt dopaminergic tone in ways that look exactly like low testosterone from the outside.

 A few signals that point toward this bucket rather than hormonal:

- Libido drops in parallel with a stressful work period, a newborn, or a training block you didn't recover from.

- You're sleeping under 6 hours, or your sleep is fragmented. One week of restricting sleep to 5 hours/night drops testosterone roughly 10–15% in healthy young men (Leproult & Van Cauter, JAMA 2011) — and the subjective effect on libido is larger than the lab number suggests.

- Morning erections are still present, occasionally. Functional plumbing with absent desire usually points away from a pure hormonal cause.

- Alcohol intake has crept up. Even moderate regular drinking suppresses both testosterone and desire.

 This bucket doesn't get fixed with a prescription. It gets fixed with sleep, training load adjustment, and — uncomfortably often — less alcohol.

 {callout: The clinical bottom line} If your testosterone is in range and your libido is low, the answer is almost never more testosterone — it's usually sleep, an SSRI, prolactin, or a metabolic problem hiding in plain sight.

### When it's neither — the bucket most clinics skip

 This is where the interesting cases live.

 **SSRIs and SNRIs.** Sexual side effects — including flat libido — affect somewhere between 25% and 70% of patients on SSRIs depending on the agent and how you ask (Higgins et al., Drug Healthc Patient Saf 2010). Bupropion and mirtazapine are notably gentler. If your libido tanked within weeks of starting an SSRI, that's your answer, and it's a conversation with your prescribing clinician — not a testosterone problem.

 **Finasteride.** A subset of men report persistent libido and erectile changes on finasteride, sometimes lingering after stopping. The size of this effect is debated; the existence isn't. If you started finasteride and libido dropped, name it as a variable.

 **Opioids and benzodiazepines.** Both suppress the HPG axis. Chronic use lowers testosterone meaningfully.

 **Metabolic disease.** Type 2 diabetes, obesity, and obstructive sleep apnea all lower testosterone and independently suppress libido and erectile function. The libido problem is real; treating it without addressing the metabolic substrate doesn't work for long.

 **Vascular and pelvic causes.** When erections become unreliable, many men quietly lose desire because sex starts to feel like a test. The libido complaint is real, but the root cause is vascular or pelvic, not hormonal.

 **Relationship context.** This one gets dismissed and shouldn't be. Desire in long-term relationships is responsive — it shows up after the conditions for intimacy do, not before. A protocol won't fix a context problem.

### How to actually work this up — tuned to your numbers

 A reasonable diagnostic sequence for a man presenting with low libido:

 1. **Timeline.** When did it change? What else changed in that window — job, sleep, a new medication, a training block, a relationship shift? 2. **Medication review.** Every prescription and OTC from the last year, including SSRIs, finasteride, opioids, and beta blockers. 3. **Sleep and alcohol.** Honest numbers, not the version you'd tell your in-laws. 4. **Labs.** Two morning testosterone draws (total and free), SHBG, LH, FSH, prolactin, sensitive estradiol, TSH, A1c, fasting glucose, lipids, and a CBC/CMP. If you choose to run bloodwork through us, these are the markers worth looking at first. 5. **Erectile function check.** Are morning erections present? Does function track with desire, or are they dissociated?

 The goal is to identify which bucket you're in before a prescription, not after.

### What the right protocol looks like once you know — matched to the actual cause

- Confirmed hypogonadism with symptoms: testosterone therapy is reasonable, with standard published titration ranges and ongoing monitoring of hematocrit, estradiol, and PSA. A clinician reviewing your symptoms, history, and labs is how dose gets matched to a person rather than to a number.

- Elevated prolactin: workup for cause (medications, pituitary imaging if indicated) before anything else.

- SSRI-driven: conversation with the prescriber about switching agents, dose timing, or adjunct strategies.

- Sleep, stress, alcohol, overtraining: behavioral first. These respond — often within weeks — and no medication outperforms fixing them.

- Metabolic: weight loss (including GLP-1 therapy where appropriate), sleep apnea evaluation, and glycemic control. Testosterone often rises 30–50 ng/dL with meaningful weight loss alone.

- Erectile component: PDE5 inhibitors can restore confidence and, indirectly, desire.

### The honest summary

 Low libido is a signal, not a diagnosis. About a third of the men who ask us about testosterone turn out to need it. The rest need a different conversation — about sleep, an SSRI, a prolactin level, or a metabolic problem that's been quietly running the show.

 The right next step isn't a prescription. It's a clear timeline, an honest medication list, and a set of labs that can tell you which bucket you're actually in.
