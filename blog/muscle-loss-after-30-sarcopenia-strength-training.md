## Muscle Loss After 30: What Sarcopenia Is and What Helps

 Research estimates adults lose roughly 3% to 8% of muscle mass per decade after 30, and the pace picks up after 60. Strength training on two or more days a week is the approach with the most evidence behind it.

 Research estimates adults lose roughly 3% to 8% of muscle mass per decade after 30, with a faster decline after 60. The age-related loss of muscle strength and mass is called sarcopenia. US guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week for adults.

 Muscle loss is usually thought of as a problem of old age. The process starts decades earlier, and it is slow enough that most people do not notice it until something that used to be easy is not.

### How much muscle is lost, and when

 A widely cited review in Current Opinion in Clinical Nutrition and Metabolic Care estimates that muscle mass falls by roughly 3% to 8% per decade after age 30, and that the rate of decline is higher after 60.

 Those are averages across populations. The pace for any one person depends on activity, nutrition, illness, hormones and genetics. Long periods of inactivity, such as bed rest after surgery or an injury, can speed it up sharply.

 Strength tends to fall faster than size. A person can look much the same and still be measurably weaker than they were ten years earlier.

### What sarcopenia means

 Sarcopenia is the medical term for age-related loss of muscle strength and mass. The revised European consensus published in Age and Ageing in 2019 puts strength first: low muscle strength is the main sign, low muscle quantity or quality confirms it, and poor physical performance marks it as severe.

 That ordering matters. It reflects evidence that strength predicts outcomes such as falls, loss of independence and hospital stays better than muscle size alone.

 Clinicians screen for it with simple tests, such as grip strength, the time it takes to stand up from a chair five times, and walking speed. A diagnosis is a clinical judgment, not something a bathroom scale or a smartwatch can make.

### Why muscle matters beyond how it looks

 Muscle does more than move the body.

- Blood sugar. Skeletal muscle is the main place glucose goes after a meal. More working muscle gives glucose more places to go.

- Balance and falls. Leg strength and power are closely tied to staying steady and recovering from a stumble.

- Bone. The pull of muscle on bone during loaded movement is one of the signals that helps maintain bone density.

- Reserve. Illness, surgery and bed rest all cost muscle. Starting with more leaves more to draw on.

 The short version

 Losing some muscle with age is expected. How much is lost is not fixed. Strength training is the intervention with the most evidence for slowing the loss, and adults respond to it at any age.

### What the evidence says helps

 **Resistance training.** A 2011 meta-analysis in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise pooled 49 studies with 1,328 adults aged 50 and over. Resistance training produced an average gain of about 1.1 kg of lean body mass. Programs with more total training volume produced larger gains, and older participants gained somewhat less than younger ones, which is an argument for starting sooner.

 **The guideline.** The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, summarized in JAMA in 2018, recommend muscle-strengthening activities of moderate or greater intensity that involve all major muscle groups on two or more days a week. For older adults they add balance training.

 **What a session looks like.** Programs in the research are built around a small number of movement patterns:

- A squat or sit-to-stand

- A hinge, such as a deadlift or hip bridge

- A push, such as a push-up or overhead press

- A pull, such as a row

- A loaded carry

 Beginners in these studies typically start with light loads or body weight and add load gradually over weeks. Consistency across months matters more than any single hard session.

 **Protein and overall intake.** Muscle is built from protein, and eating too little of it, or too little food overall, makes it harder to keep. Needs vary with age, body size, kidney function and activity, so the right amount is a question for a clinician or dietitian.

### Weight loss and muscle

 When body weight comes down, some of what is lost is usually lean tissue, not only fat. That applies to dieting and to weight-loss medication alike. Strength training and adequate protein during weight loss are the standard ways clinicians try to protect muscle. ### Who should check with a clinician first

 Strength training is safe for most adults when it is built up gradually. A medical check before starting is sensible for people with heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, recent surgery, osteoporosis with previous fractures, or joint pain that limits movement.

 Unexplained weakness, rapid loss of muscle, or repeated falls should be assessed by a clinician. These can have causes other than age, including thyroid problems, low vitamin D, medication effects and nerve conditions.

### The takeaway

 Muscle is one of the few parts of aging that responds clearly to effort. Two sessions a week of basic strength work, kept up for years, is a small commitment relative to what it protects.
