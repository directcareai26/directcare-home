Omega-3 EPA/DHA: The Dose That actually moves Your Inflammation Markers

Most over-the-counter fish oil bottles deliver too little EPA/DHA to shift inflammation biomarkers like hs-CRP or the Omega-3 Index. Here's the dose range the trials actually used — and how to know if yours is working.

If you've been taking a 1,000 mg fish oil softgel every morning and wondering why your hs-CRP hasn't budged, the answer is usually simple: the dose is too low, and most of that 1,000 mg isn't even EPA or DHA. The trials that actually moved inflammation markers used gram-level doses of the active omega-3s — not the total capsule weight on the label.

This is one of the most common supplement mistakes we see in patient intake notes. Let's walk through what the evidence actually shows, what to measure, and how to think about dosing without overshooting.

Why the label on your fish oil bottle is misleading

A "1,000 mg fish oil" capsule typically contains around 180 mg EPA and 120 mg DHA — roughly 300 mg of the omega-3s that do the anti-inflammatory work. The other 700 mg is other fatty acids and filler oils.

So when a study says participants took "2 grams of EPA/DHA per day," that's not two of your softgels. That's closer to six or seven of them, depending on the product.

This matters because dose-response is real with omega-3s. Under-dose, and nothing measurable happens. Get into the therapeutic range, and you can see meaningful shifts in triglycerides, the Omega-3 Index, and inflammatory markers within 8–12 weeks.

What dose actually moves hs-CRP and other inflammation markers — in plain English

The cleanest signal comes from cardiovascular and rheumatology literature. A 2020 meta-analysis in the Journal of the American Heart Association (Bernasconi et al.) pooled 40 randomized trials and found that EPA/DHA doses averaging around 2.2 g/day produced meaningful reductions in cardiovascular events, with dose-response above 1 g.

For inflammation specifically:

  • hs-CRP: Meta-analyses show statistically significant reductions typically at doses of 2–3 g/day combined EPA/DHA, with effects most consistent in patients whose baseline hs-CRP was elevated (>2 mg/L).
  • IL-6 and TNF-α: Similar dose range, 2–3 g/day, with 8–12 weeks of consistent intake.
  • Triglycerides: The clearest dose-response. Roughly a 25–30% reduction at 4 g/day, which is the FDA-approved prescription range (icosapent ethyl and omega-3-acid ethyl esters).
  • Omega-3 Index (red blood cell EPA+DHA %): Moving from a typical Western baseline of ~4% into the cardioprotective target of 8–12% generally requires 1.5–2 g/day of EPA/DHA for 3–4 months.

So the honest answer to "what dose works" is: for inflammation, plan on 2–3 grams per day of combined EPA + DHA, not total fish oil weight. For triglyceride lowering, higher. For general maintenance in someone already eating fatty fish twice a week, less.

The labs worth having before you start a serious omega-3 protocol

If you're going to take a supplement seriously enough to hit gram-level doses, it's worth knowing your starting point so you can tell if it's working. The markers worth having on a baseline panel:

  • Omega-3 Index (red blood cell EPA + DHA as % of total fatty acids) — the single best marker of tissue-level omega-3 status.
  • hs-CRP — general inflammation.
  • Fasting triglycerides — most sensitive to omega-3 dose.
  • ApoB or a full lipid panel — because high-dose EPA/DHA can nudge LDL-C in some people, and you want to know.

Retest at 12 weeks. If your Omega-3 Index hasn't moved from 4% to at least 6–8%, either the dose is too low or absorption is poor (see next section).

{callout: The bottom line} For inflammation, the trials that worked used 2–3 grams per day of combined EPA + DHA — not 1,000 mg of total fish oil. Check your label's EPA and DHA numbers, not the capsule size.

Triglyceride form vs. ethyl ester vs. phospholipid — what actually changes

Omega-3s come in different molecular forms, and absorption varies:

  • Ethyl ester (EE): The cheapest and most common form in generic fish oil. Absorption is roughly 20–30% lower than triglyceride form when taken on an empty stomach, but nearly equivalent when taken with a fat-containing meal.
  • Re-esterified triglyceride (rTG): Better absorbed, more expensive.
  • Phospholipid form (krill oil): Well absorbed at lower doses, but krill capsules typically contain only 100–150 mg EPA/DHA each — you'd need a lot of them to hit 2 g/day.

Practical take: form matters less than taking it with a meal that contains fat. That single habit closes most of the absorption gap between ethyl ester and triglyceride forms.

What about prescription omega-3s?

Two FDA-approved prescription products exist:

  • Icosapent ethyl (pure EPA, 4 g/day) — studied in REDUCE-IT, showed a 25% reduction in major cardiovascular events in patients with elevated triglycerides on a statin.
  • Omega-3-acid ethyl esters (EPA + DHA combination, 4 g/day).

These are appropriate when you're treating triglycerides >500 mg/dL or high cardiovascular risk with elevated TGs on statin therapy — a clinical decision, not a supplement decision. For most people looking to shift inflammation markers, high-quality OTC EPA/DHA at 2–3 g/day is a reasonable starting point, ideally reviewed alongside your other labs and medications.

Side effects and interactions at gram-level doses

Omega-3s are well tolerated, but at 2+ g/day you should know:

  • Bleeding risk: Real but modest. If you're on warfarin, a DOAC, or dual antiplatelet therapy, talk to your prescriber before starting.
  • Atrial fibrillation: STRENGTH and REDUCE-IT both showed a small but statistically significant increase in new-onset AF at 4 g/day. The absolute risk is low, but it's a reason not to megadose casually.
  • Fishy reflux: Usually solved by taking with food, splitting the dose, or switching to enteric-coated capsules.
  • LDL-C: DHA in particular can raise LDL-C modestly in some patients. This is another reason to have baseline lipids.
Dose-response is real with omega-3s. Under-dose, and nothing measurable happens. Hit the therapeutic range, and the biomarkers move.

How to structure a 12-week omega-3 trial on yourself

If you want to know whether omega-3s are doing anything measurable for you, run it like a small experiment:

1. Baseline labs: Omega-3 Index, hs-CRP, fasting triglycerides, full lipid panel. 2. Dose: Start at 2 g/day combined EPA + DHA (read the label — add the EPA and DHA milligrams, ignore total fish oil weight). Take with a meal containing fat. 3. Consistency: Daily, for 12 weeks. Omega-3s incorporate into cell membranes slowly; you won't see the effect in two weeks. 4. Retest at 12 weeks: Same labs. If Omega-3 Index is >8% and hs-CRP dropped, you've got your answer. If not, either the dose was too low, absorption is poor, or the underlying inflammation isn't diet-responsive.

This is the kind of workup a clinician would typically run alongside a broader look at cardiovascular risk — cheap, low-risk, and it gives you actual numbers instead of a vague sense that you're "doing something healthy."

The short version

  • 1,000 mg fish oil ≠ 1,000 mg EPA/DHA. Read the label.
  • 2–3 g/day combined EPA + DHA is the range that moves inflammation markers in the trials.
  • Take it with a fatty meal. Doubles absorption for ethyl ester forms.
  • Measure at baseline and 12 weeks: Omega-3 Index, hs-CRP, triglycerides.
  • Above 4 g/day, the cardiovascular signal gets more complicated (AF risk, LDL-C shifts) — that's clinician territory.

Omega-3s aren't a magic anti-inflammatory. They're one of the few supplements where the dose-response is well-characterized and the biomarker you're trying to move is directly measurable. That's a rare combination in the supplement world, and worth taking advantage of.
