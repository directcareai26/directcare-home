## One Night of Short Sleep and Insulin Sensitivity : The Data

 In a 2010 study, healthy adults who slept four hours for a single night had measurably lower insulin sensitivity the next day. Here is what the research shows, what it does not, and why it runs both ways.

 In a 2010 study of healthy adults, a single night of about four hours of sleep reduced insulin sensitivity the following day compared with a normal night. Larger observational studies link habitual short sleep with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes. Sleep experts recommend seven or more hours for adults.

 Most people think of a bad night as a problem for mood and focus. Research over the past 25 years shows it also changes how the body handles sugar, and the change shows up faster than expected.

### What insulin sensitivity is, in plain English

 Insulin is the hormone that moves glucose out of the blood and into cells. Insulin sensitivity describes how well that works. When sensitivity is high, a small amount of insulin does the job. When it falls, the body needs more insulin to move the same amount of glucose.

 Lower sensitivity is called insulin resistance. Over years, it is one of the main routes to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.

### What the 2010 study did

 Researchers in the Netherlands studied nine healthy adults on two occasions: once after a normal night of about eight and a half hours in bed, and once after a night cut to four hours. The morning after each night, they measured insulin sensitivity with a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, the most precise research method for it.

 After the short night, the body's ability to clear glucose in response to insulin fell by roughly a quarter, and the liver released more glucose than it had after the full night. The results were published in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism.

 This was one night, in people with no metabolic disease.

 Keep it in proportion

 This was a small laboratory study, and the effect was measured the next morning. It shows the system responds quickly to lost sleep. It does not show that one bad night does lasting harm.

### What happens when short sleep becomes the habit

 An earlier experiment published in The Lancet in 1999 restricted 11 healthy young men to four hours in bed for six nights. Their glucose tolerance fell, and evening levels of the stress hormone cortisol rose. The changes reversed after recovery sleep.

 Outside the lab, a 2010 meta-analysis in Diabetes Care pooled 10 studies following more than 107,000 adults. People who habitually slept five to six hours or less had about a 28% higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes than those sleeping around seven to eight hours. Long sleep and poor-quality sleep were linked with higher risk too.

 Those are observational findings. Short sleepers differ from longer sleepers in other ways, including work schedules, stress and health conditions, so the figure describes an association and not a proven cause.

### Why sleep would affect blood sugar

 Several mechanisms have been proposed, and they probably act together:

- Higher evening cortisol and more activity in the stress-response system, both of which oppose insulin

- Changes in the hormones that govern hunger and fullness, which can raise appetite

- Less deep sleep, the stage most closely tied to glucose regulation in experiments

- More waking hours in which to eat, often late in the evening

### It moves the other way too

 In the experimental studies, the changes in glucose handling improved after participants caught up on sleep. That is the practical point. Sleep is one of the inputs to metabolic health that can be changed.

 A joint consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommends that adults sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.

 Habits that sleep researchers commonly recommend include:

- A consistent wake time, including weekends

- A cool, dark and quiet bedroom

- Screens and phones out of the bedroom, or at least out of reach

- Caffeine kept to the earlier part of the day

- Alcohol kept modest, since it fragments sleep later in the night

### When to talk with a clinician

 Sleep habits do not fix everything. Some sleep problems are medical and deserve an assessment:

- Loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing during sleep, which can indicate sleep apnea

- Trouble falling or staying asleep at least three nights a week for three months or more

- Sleepiness that makes driving or work unsafe

- Restless or uncomfortable legs in the evening

 Sleep apnea in particular is linked with insulin resistance and high blood pressure, and it is treatable.

### How it shows up in bloodwork

 There is no blood test for a bad night. Over time, though, the effect of poor sleep can contribute to the markers clinicians use to track metabolic health: fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides and, in some cases, fasting insulin.
