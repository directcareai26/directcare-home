Micronized Progesterone vs. Progestins: why it matters

Oral micronized progesterone and synthetic progestins are not interchangeable — they diverge on breast cancer signal, sleep, and neurosteroid activity. Here's what the evidence actually says.

If you're on estrogen therapy and still have a uterus, you need a progestogen. That part isn't controversial. What is controversial — and clinically important — is which progestogen, because oral micronized progesterone (the molecule your ovaries actually made) and synthetic progestins like medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) or norethindrone behave differently in breast tissue, brain tissue, and the vascular wall.

This matters because the WHI trial that scared a generation of women off HRT in 2002 used MPA, not progesterone. The distinction was buried for years. It shouldn't be.

What counts as a "progestogen," in plain English

"Progestogen" is the umbrella term. Under it sit two very different groups:

  • Progesterone — the identical molecule to what your corpus luteum secretes. Prescribed as oral micronized progesterone (Prometrium is the branded form) or as compounded formulations.
  • Progestins — synthetic molecules designed to bind the progesterone receptor. MPA, norethindrone acetate, levonorgestrel, drospirenone, and others. Each has its own receptor promiscuity — some also hit androgen, glucocorticoid, or mineralocorticoid receptors.

Both categories will do the core job in HRT: oppose estrogen's proliferative effect on the endometrium and prevent endometrial hyperplasia. That's the non-negotiable reason a progestogen is prescribed alongside systemic estrogen in a woman with a uterus.

Where they diverge is everywhere else.

The breast cancer signal: what actually changes

The cleanest data here comes from the French E3N cohort, which followed more than 80,000 postmenopausal women. Women using estrogen plus micronized progesterone showed no statistically significant increase in breast cancer risk over roughly five years of use, while women using estrogen plus a synthetic progestin showed a relative risk around 1.4 (Fournier et al., Breast Cancer Research and Treatment, 2008).

The KEEPS and ELITE trials added mechanistic weight: micronized progesterone appears to have a more neutral proliferative profile in breast tissue than MPA does. The 2020 Collaborative Group meta-analysis in The Lancet also flagged that all progestogens carry some breast signal with long-term use, but the magnitude and time course differ — and micronized progesterone consistently sits at the lower end.

A fair read: micronized progesterone is not risk-free, but the risk curve is meaningfully flatter than MPA's, especially in the first five years.

The WHI didn't indict progesterone. It indicted medroxyprogesterone acetate. We spent 20 years conflating the two.

{callout: The key takeaway} If a woman on estrogen HRT needs endometrial protection, oral micronized progesterone has a more favorable breast and cardiovascular profile than MPA in the available data — and it's the default first-line choice for most women without a specific reason to use a synthetic progestin.

Brain and sleep: the quiet advantage

Oral micronized progesterone has a party trick that progestins don't: when metabolized in the liver, it produces allopregnanolone, a neurosteroid that positively modulates GABA-A receptors. In practice, this is why patients taking it at bedtime often report better sleep continuity and a calmer nervous system — effects documented in small randomized trials (Caufriez et al., Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 2011).

Progestins don't generate allopregnanolone in meaningful amounts. Some, like MPA, may actually blunt estrogen's beneficial effects on hippocampal function in animal models, though the human data is still evolving.

This is why the standard prescribing pattern for oral micronized progesterone is at bedtime — you want the sedating metabolite working with you, not making you groggy at 10 a.m.

Cardiovascular and metabolic differences

MPA appears to partially counteract estrogen's favorable effects on HDL and endothelial function. Micronized progesterone, in trials like PEPI, was more metabolically neutral. Drospirenone has a mild anti-mineralocorticoid effect (mild diuretic behavior) that can be useful in women who retain water on estrogen. Norethindrone has weak androgenic activity that some women feel as skin or mood changes.

None of this makes progestins "bad." It makes them different tools with different profiles. A woman with heavy bleeding on cyclic HRT may do better on a levonorgestrel IUD for local endometrial control. A woman with primary insomnia and anxiety may do dramatically better on oral micronized progesterone at bedtime.

Cyclic vs. continuous — the dosing question

Two standard regimens sit within published guidelines:

  • Continuous combined: progesterone taken every night alongside daily estrogen. Typical published range for oral micronized progesterone is 100 mg nightly. Goal: no bleeding, steady state.
  • Cyclic: higher dose (typically 200 mg) for 12–14 days per month, mimicking a luteal phase. Produces a scheduled withdrawal bleed. Often preferred in perimenopause or in women closer to their final menstrual period.

Which regimen fits you depends on where you are in the menopause transition, whether you want to bleed, and how your endometrium responds. This is a conversation with a clinician, not a self-select.

Who might still be better on a synthetic progestin

Micronized progesterone isn't automatically the right answer for everyone:

  • Peanut allergy: the branded oral formulation is suspended in peanut oil. Compounded versions or a levonorgestrel IUD are alternatives.
  • Heavy or unpredictable bleeding: a levonorgestrel IUD delivers local endometrial suppression more reliably than oral progesterone in some patients.
  • Contraception still needed in late perimenopause: a combined oral contraceptive or progestin-only method may cover both needs at once.
  • Poor tolerance to sedation or dizziness: some women feel too groggy on oral micronized progesterone even at bedtime. Vaginal micronized progesterone is an option that bypasses first-pass metabolism.

The labs worth having before a serious HRT protocol

HRT decisions get better when a clinician can see the surrounding context — symptoms, history, and, when you've run them, labs. The markers worth having on the table before starting or adjusting a regimen typically include:

  • FSH and estradiol — to confirm menopausal status if it's ambiguous.
  • TSH and free T4 — thyroid symptoms overlap heavily with menopausal symptoms.
  • Lipid panel and fasting glucose / HbA1c — baseline cardiometabolic picture.
  • Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel — general safety baseline.
  • Vitamin D, ferritin — commonly low and commonly symptomatic.

None of these are gatekeepers to a conversation. They're context that makes the conversation sharper.

The practical bottom line

For most women on systemic estrogen who need endometrial protection, oral micronized progesterone is the first-line choice on the strength of its breast-tissue profile, its neurosteroid-driven sleep benefit, and its cardiovascular neutrality. Synthetic progestins are legitimate tools for specific clinical situations — but they're no longer the default, and they shouldn't be prescribed as if they were interchangeable with progesterone.

If you're already on an estrogen-plus-MPA regimen and doing well, that's not an emergency. It's a conversation worth having at your next visit — not a reason to stop anything on your own.

HRT is one of the highest-leverage decisions in midlife health. The molecule matters. The dose matters. The route matters. And the person prescribing it should be able to explain, in plain English, why they picked what they picked.
