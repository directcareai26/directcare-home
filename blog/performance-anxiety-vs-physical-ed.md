## Performance Anxiety vs Physical ED: how clinicians tell

 Performance anxiety and physical erectile dysfunction can look identical in the bedroom but behave very differently in a clinical workup. Here is how a careful history, a few questions, and targeted labs usually separate the two.

 Clinicians separate performance anxiety from physical ED using pattern recognition: psychogenic ED typically appears suddenly, is situational, and preserves nighttime and morning erections, while organic ED tends to progress gradually and affects all contexts. A focused history, validated questionnaires, and targeted labs usually clarify which driver is dominant.

 Most men who walk into an ED visit have already Googled it, blamed themselves, and ruled out the wrong thing. The clinical question is almost never "is this real?" — it is "is this mostly in the nervous system, mostly in the blood vessels, or both?" Those three answers lead to three different plans.

 Erectile dysfunction is common, treatable, and almost always worth a workup rather than a guess. The AUA guideline frames it as a shared-decision condition where history alone resolves most cases, and a short lab panel resolves most of the rest.

### What "performance anxiety" actually means in plain English

 Performance anxiety is a sympathetic nervous system response — the same fight-or-flight circuitry that dilates your pupils and tightens your gut. Erections depend on parasympathetic dominance and smooth muscle relaxation in the penile arteries. The two systems are mutually exclusive at the vascular level, which is why anxiety is such a reliable off-switch.

 This is sometimes called psychogenic ED. It is not "fake" ED. It is a real failure of erection driven by a real neurochemical cascade — just one that originates above the neck.

### What "physical" or organic ED actually means

 Organic ED is a plumbing or signaling problem. The usual suspects:

- Vascular: atherosclerosis of the small penile arteries, often the earliest sign of systemic cardiovascular disease

- Endocrine: low testosterone, thyroid dysfunction, elevated prolactin, poorly controlled diabetes

- Neurogenic: pelvic surgery, spinal injury, diabetic neuropathy, multiple sclerosis

- Medication-induced: SSRIs, beta blockers, finasteride in a subset of patients, opioids

- Lifestyle: heavy alcohol, nicotine, sleep deprivation, untreated sleep apnea

 The Nature Reviews Primer on ED is a good single reference here — it maps how often each driver shows up in a general urology clinic.

### The question that does most of the work

 "Do you still get erections in your sleep or when you wake up — even if they are not as firm as they used to be?"
 If the answer is a clear yes, and erections also occur with masturbation but fail with a partner, the pattern is strongly psychogenic. The hardware is working; the context is the trigger.

 If the answer is no — nighttime and morning erections have faded gradually over months or years, and the problem shows up in every context — the pattern leans organic.

 Mixed pictures are common. A man with mild early vascular ED often develops performance anxiety on top of it, and the anxiety becomes the louder voice.

 **The clinical shortcut**Sudden onset, situational, preserved morning erections = usually psychogenic. Gradual onset, present in every context, absent morning erections = usually organic. Most real cases sit somewhere on that spectrum.

### The structured history tuned to your pattern

 A careful intake walks through:

- Onset: sudden (hours to days) suggests psychogenic; gradual (months to years) suggests organic

- Situational variability: fine alone, fails with a partner → psychogenic signal

- Nocturnal and morning erections: present → favors psychogenic; absent → favors organic

- Relationship context: new partner, relationship stress, recent loss of confidence after one bad episode

- Medication timeline: did symptoms start within weeks of a new SSRI, beta blocker, or finasteride prescription?

- Cardiovascular risk: hypertension, lipids, smoking history, waist circumference

- Mood and sleep: depression, anxiety disorder, insomnia, suspected sleep apnea

 The validated tool here is the IIEF-5 (also called SHIM), a five-question screener that stratifies ED severity and gives a baseline to measure change against. It does not distinguish cause, but it anchors the conversation.

### The labs worth having before a serious protocol

 If you choose to do bloodwork as part of a workup, the markers that most often change management:

- Total and free testosterone (morning draw, ideally repeated if low) — the Endocrine Society guideline is specific about timing and confirmation

- LH and FSH to localize low testosterone to the testis vs the pituitary

- Prolactin if testosterone is low or libido is disproportionately affected

- TSH for thyroid contribution

- Fasting glucose and HbA1c — diabetes is one of the most common organic drivers

- Lipid panel — ED is often the first visible sign of endothelial disease

- CBC and metabolic panel as a baseline before any therapy

 None of this is a prerequisite to a conversation. It is the panel a clinician would typically want in hand before committing to a long-term protocol, especially if testosterone therapy is on the table.

### Why the distinction changes the plan

 For predominantly psychogenic ED, first-line options evidence-based literature supports include cognitive behavioral therapy, sex therapy, and short-term use of a PDE5 inhibitor to break the anxiety-failure loop. The medication is often a bridge, not a destination.

 For predominantly organic ED, the plan addresses the underlying driver: cardiovascular risk reduction, glucose control, medication review, testosterone replacement if genuinely hypogonadal (not just low-normal), and PDE5 inhibitors as symptomatic therapy. The AUA guideline is explicit that PDE5 inhibitors are first-line pharmacotherapy for most men regardless of cause, assuming no nitrate use or other contraindication.

 Mixed presentations — the majority, in practice — get a combined plan. Treat the vascular or hormonal driver, treat the anxiety loop, and reassess at a defined interval rather than guessing.

### What a careful workup actually changes

 A 32-year-old with new situational ED after a job loss and preserved morning erections does not need testosterone. A 58-year-old with gradual loss of nighttime erections, a 38-inch waist, and borderline HbA1c does not need a therapist as his first stop. Both are common. Both get missed when ED is treated as a single problem with a single pill.

 The point of separating performance anxiety from physical ED is not to assign blame to the mind or the body. It is to make sure the treatment matches the mechanism — and that the mechanism gets identified before months of the wrong plan go by.
