2. Endocrinologist: best for complex hormone findings

An endocrinologist evaluates disorders involving hormones and the glands that produce them. This route is particularly relevant when low testosterone appears alongside other abnormal hormone results or needs investigation beyond replacement therapy.

Low testosterone is a finding; it is not always the full diagnosis. The useful question is why it is low.

Where an endocrinologist shines

  • Investigates hormone-related causes of testosterone deficiency.
  • Can assess whether pituitary or testicular signaling needs further evaluation.
  • Helps distinguish a replacement-treatment question from a broader endocrine problem.

Where an endocrinologist falls short

  • Specialist evaluation is not a substitute for urgent assessment of sudden or severe symptoms.
  • Not every endocrinology practice focuses on adult male reproductive hormones.
  • A specialist visit does not guarantee that testosterone treatment is indicated.

3. Urologist: best for fertility and testicular concerns

A urologist is a relevant alternative when testosterone concerns overlap with reproductive health. Fertility goals, testicular symptoms, and some sexual-health problems change what a useful evaluation needs to cover.

This distinction matters because externally supplied testosterone can suppress sperm production. A discussion about future pregnancy plans belongs before treatment, not after it starts.

Where a urologist shines

  • Evaluates testicular and male reproductive concerns.
  • Can address fertility questions alongside testosterone symptoms.
  • Provides an in-person route when physical examination is necessary.

Where a urologist falls short

  • Not every urologist specializes in reproductive medicine or testosterone management.
  • A urology appointment does not make every erectile problem a testosterone problem.
  • Hormone findings involving other endocrine conditions can require additional specialist input.

Best for: People whose testosterone concerns include fertility goals or testicular findings.

Verdict: Skip a TRT-first approach until fertility goals and relevant physical symptoms are addressed.

4. Primary care: best for a broad first assessment

A primary-care clinician is a practical starting point when your main complaint is tiredness, reduced libido, or a general decline in how you feel. The job is to assess the whole picture before narrowing the explanation to testosterone.

Bring your medication list, sleep history, previous results, and a clear description of when symptoms started. Those details help the clinician decide what testing and referrals are appropriate.

Where primary care shines

  • Assesses common alternative explanations for nonspecific symptoms.
  • Can put new symptoms in the context of existing conditions and medications.
  • Provides a route to specialist referral when the findings call for it.

Where primary care falls short

  • Individual clinicians differ in whether they manage TRT themselves.
  • Complicated hormone or fertility findings can require specialist care.

Best for: People who need an initial assessment rather than a predetermined treatment.

Verdict: Hold off on choosing a testosterone prescription before completing a broad evaluation.

What a sound TRT evaluation includes

A useful comparison of providers starts with the clinical process. In 2026, a polished website still cannot replace a diagnosis supported by symptoms and appropriate testing.

Symptoms

Explain what changed and how it affects you. Reduced libido, fewer spontaneous erections, and other symptoms deserve assessment, but none establishes testosterone deficiency on its own.

Morning testing

The American Urological Association recommends diagnosing low testosterone only after 2 total-testosterone measurements on separate early mornings, together with symptoms or signs. Testosterone varies, so a single result is not enough.

The association uses below 300 ng/dL as a reasonable total-testosterone cutoff supporting the diagnosis. That threshold is not permission to self-prescribe; your clinician must interpret the result in context.

Cause assessment

Ask what the results suggest about the source of the problem. Depending on the findings, clinicians use additional testing, including luteinizing hormone, to help distinguish different causes.

Also disclose fertility goals, sleep apnea, medications, and relevant medical history. These details affect the decision, not just the paperwork.

Monitoring

Ask how the clinician will review symptoms, testosterone levels, and blood counts if treatment begins. The Endocrine Society's testosterone-therapy guidance identifies a hematocrit above 54% as a reason to stop treatment temporarily and evaluate contributing factors under medical supervision.

Hematocrit describes how much of your blood consists of red blood cells. Do not change a prescribed dose yourself after reading a threshold online.

Questions to ask before you commit

For your 2026 shortlist, ask every provider the same questions. Clear answers make different care routes easier to compare.

  • Who makes the diagnosis and reviews the lab results?
  • How are repeat morning testosterone measurements handled?
  • What findings require an in-person visit or specialist referral?

  • What happens if symptoms do not improve despite a changed testosterone level?
  • Who responds to side effects, and how is follow-up arranged?

Choose the provider that explains the diagnostic reasoning, not the provider that treats a prescription as the inevitable outcome. A decision against TRT can be an appropriate result of a good evaluation.

FAQ

What's the best Ro alternative for TRT in 2026?

DirectCare AI is a relevant telehealth alternative because its stated services include TRT and blood lab testing. An endocrinologist is a stronger fit when you need specialist investigation of complex hormone findings.

Does Ro provide the testosterone treatment I need?

Confirm Ro's current testosterone-related services directly before choosing it for TRT. A general men's-health service listing does not establish which evaluation or treatment pathway applies to you.

Do I need two testosterone tests before starting TRT?

The American Urological Association recommends 2 total-testosterone measurements on separate early mornings to establish low testosterone. Diagnosis also requires relevant symptoms or signs, not a lab result alone.

Is a testosterone level below 300 ng/dL enough to start treatment?

No, a result below 300 ng/dL does not establish that you should start TRT. The American Urological Association uses that cutoff to support diagnosis alongside repeat morning testing and relevant symptoms or signs.

Should I see a urologist if I want children?

A urologist with reproductive-health expertise is a relevant option when fertility matters. Externally supplied testosterone can suppress sperm production, so discuss pregnancy plans before treatment.

Can telehealth replace an in-person testosterone evaluation?

Telehealth cannot replace every part of an in-person evaluation. A testicular lump, examination finding, or other concern requiring physical assessment needs an appropriate in-person care route.

What should I compare besides the treatment itself?

Compare diagnostic requirements, clinician responsibility, monitoring, and referral arrangements. A suitable TRT service explains what happens before treatment, during follow-up, and when a problem needs different care.

One last thing

A higher testosterone result is not the same as a successful treatment outcome. If symptoms persist, the next step is a clinical review—not an automatic dose increase.

For a 2026 decision, keep one question at the center: What evidence would make this clinician recommend against TRT? The answer tells you whether you are choosing an evaluation or simply expecting a prescription.
