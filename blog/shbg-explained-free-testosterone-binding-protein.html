SHBG Explained: the protein that gates your testosterone

SHBG is the liver protein that decides how much of your testosterone actually reaches tissue. Understanding it is often the difference between a 'normal' lab and a symptom picture that still makes sense.

SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin) is a liver-made protein that binds tightly to testosterone in the blood. Only testosterone not bound to SHBG is available to tissues. SHBG levels can shift with age, thyroid status, insulin resistance, and oral estrogens, which may change how a given total testosterone number actually feels.

If you've ever looked at a testosterone panel where the total number looked fine but you still felt flat, SHBG is usually the missing piece of the story. It's the protein that decides how much of your testosterone is actually free to do its job.

This is one of those labs that quietly runs the show. A clinician reading your panel without it is reading half a page.

What SHBG actually is, in plain English

SHBG stands for sex hormone-binding globulin. It's a glycoprotein made mostly by your liver that circulates in your blood and binds to sex steroids — primarily testosterone, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), and estradiol.

When testosterone is bound tightly to SHBG, it is biologically inert in that moment. It can't cross into cells and act on the androgen receptor. Only the fraction that is free, plus the fraction loosely bound to albumin, is considered bioavailable — i.e., actually usable by tissue.

In a typical adult man, roughly 40–60% of circulating testosterone is bound to SHBG, around 40–50% is loosely bound to albumin, and only about 1–3% floats around truly free. That tiny free fraction is what your brain, muscle, bone, and genital tissue mostly care about.

Why total testosterone can lie to you

Total testosterone is a sum: free + albumin-bound + SHBG-bound. If SHBG is unusually high, your total can look reassuring while your free fraction is low and you feel every symptom of low T. If SHBG is unusually low — common in insulin resistance and obesity — your total can look mediocre while your free is actually adequate.

The Endocrine Society's clinical practice guideline for testosterone therapy in men with hypogonadism specifically recommends measuring free or bioavailable testosterone when SHBG is suspected to be abnormal, because total alone can be misleading in those settings.

A "normal" total testosterone with an abnormal SHBG is not a normal panel. It is an incomplete one.

What drives SHBG up or down, and why it matters

SHBG is not a fixed trait. It shifts with physiology, meds, and metabolic health.

Things that tend to raise SHBG:

  • Aging (SHBG generally rises decade over decade in men)
  • Hyperthyroidism
  • Chronic liver disease and hepatitis
  • Oral estrogens (including some combined oral contraceptives in women)
  • Significant caloric restriction and low body fat
  • Certain anticonvulsants

Things that tend to lower SHBG:

  • Insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome
  • Obesity, especially visceral adiposity
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Exogenous androgens (including TRT) and anabolic steroid use
  • Growth hormone excess
  • Nephrotic syndrome

The link with insulin is especially worth sitting with. Multiple cohort analyses have shown that low SHBG tracks tightly with insulin resistance and is actually a predictor of future type 2 diabetes, independent of total testosterone. In other words, a surprisingly low SHBG in a 35-year-old man is a signal worth investigating metabolically, not just hormonally.

The pointSHBG is a gatekeeper, not a bystander — it decides what fraction of your testosterone is usable, and it is itself a readout of your metabolic and liver health.

How SHBG changes the TRT conversation

When SHBG is measured alongside total and free testosterone, three patterns show up repeatedly in clinic.

Pattern 1: Low total, low-normal SHBG. Classic primary or secondary hypogonadism picture. Free testosterone is usually low too. Decisions here lean on symptoms, repeat labs, LH/FSH, and the full workup a clinician would review.

Pattern 2: Low-normal total, high SHBG. The total looks "fine" but calculated free testosterone comes back low. The symptoms match low T even though the headline number doesn't. This is where ignoring SHBG leads to the wrong answer.

Pattern 3: Low-normal total, low SHBG. Often seen in men with higher body fat, elevated fasting insulin, or fatty liver. Free testosterone may be adequate. The highest-yield intervention here is often metabolic — sleep, resistance training, reducing visceral fat — not immediately starting an androgen.

On therapy, SHBG tends to drift down somewhat with exogenous testosterone, which is one reason free testosterone is tracked over time, not just total. The frequency and route of dosing can also change how SHBG behaves — another reason protocols are individualized rather than one-size.

How SHBG is measured, and what to actually order

SHBG is a straightforward serum immunoassay. The useful move is to order it alongside total testosterone so free testosterone can be reliably calculated (the Vermeulen equation, which most labs use, needs total T, SHBG, and albumin).

If you're building a workup for symptoms that could be hypogonadism, the labs worth having on the table typically include:

  • Total testosterone, drawn fasting between 7–10 AM, ideally on two separate mornings
  • SHBG
  • Albumin (for the free T calculation)
  • LH and FSH
  • Estradiol (sensitive assay)
  • Prolactin
  • A CBC and CMP for baseline hematocrit and liver function
  • TSH, because thyroid status pushes SHBG around meaningfully
  • A fasting glucose and HbA1c, because insulin resistance is one of the loudest SHBG suppressors

Direct measurement of free testosterone by equilibrium dialysis is the gold standard but isn't always available or necessary; a calculated free T from total + SHBG + albumin is a reasonable substitute in most settings, per the Endocrine Society guideline.

When an "abnormal" SHBG is actually the diagnosis

Sometimes SHBG isn't noise around the testosterone question — it is the finding.

A very low SHBG in a younger adult may be the first visible flag of insulin resistance before fasting glucose or A1c moves. A very high SHBG can be an early readout of thyroid overactivity or occult liver disease. Oral estrogen (including in gender-affirming regimens or in women on certain oral contraceptives) reliably pushes SHBG up, which is expected pharmacology, not pathology.

The clinical move is the same in every case: interpret SHBG as a signal from the liver and the metabolic system, not just as a number that modifies testosterone.

The bottom line

SHBG decides what fraction of your testosterone your body can actually use. A testosterone panel without SHBG is a panel with a missing variable, and the shape of your SHBG — high, low, drifting — often explains why a "normal" lab doesn't match how you feel.

If you're thinking about TRT, or trying to make sense of a confusing panel, the useful next step is a full picture: total T, SHBG, free T, LH/FSH, estradiol, thyroid, and metabolic markers, read together by a clinician who treats the pattern rather than one number.
