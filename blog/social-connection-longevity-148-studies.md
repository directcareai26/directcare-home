## Social Connection and Longevity: What 148 Studies Found

 A review of 148 studies and more than 300,000 people found that those with stronger social relationships were 50% more likely to survive over the study period.

 A 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 people found that participants with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival over the follow-up period than those with weaker relationships. The finding held across age, sex and starting health.

 Health advice tends to focus on what can be counted: steps, hours of sleep, blood pressure. Researchers at Brigham Young University pooled the evidence on something harder to count, the strength of a person's relationships.

#### What the review did

 The team combined 148 studies that had measured people's social relationships and then followed them over time. Together the studies included 308,849 people.

 The studies measured relationships in different ways. Some used a single fact, such as whether a person lived alone. Others used fuller measures of social integration: marriage, contact with friends and family, and involvement in community groups.

#### What it found

- People with stronger social relationships were 50% more likely to be alive at the end of follow-up

- The result held across age, sex, health at the start, and cause of death

- The fuller measures of social integration showed the strongest link, with odds of survival 91% higher

- Living alone versus with others, taken alone, showed the weakest link

 The authors concluded that the effect of social relationships on the risk of death is comparable with well-established risk factors.

 Association, not proof

 These were observational studies. Illness can shrink a person's social life, so some of the link runs in the other direction. The consistency across 148 studies, and across people who were healthy at the start, is what makes the finding hard to dismiss.

#### Why connection could affect health

 Several explanations have been proposed, and they likely work together:

- People close to you notice changes and push you to get care

- Shared routines such as meals and walks support healthier habits

- Support during hard periods lowers the physical load of stress

- A sense of purpose and belonging is linked with better mood and sleep
