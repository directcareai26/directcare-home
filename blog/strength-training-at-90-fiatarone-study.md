## Strength Training at 90: The Study That Changed the Rules

 In a 1990 JAMA study, frail 90-year-olds increased leg strength by an average of 174% after 8 weeks of supervised high-intensity resistance training.

 In a 1990 study published in JAMA, nine frail nursing home residents with an average age of 90 completed 8 weeks of high-intensity resistance training. Their leg strength increased by an average of 174%, mid-thigh muscle area by 9%, and tandem gait speed by 48%.

 For much of the last century, muscle loss in old age was treated as unavoidable, and heavy exercise for frail older people was considered unsafe. A small study in Boston tested that belief directly.

#### What the study did

 Ten frail residents of a long-term care facility, average age 90, volunteered for eight weeks of high-intensity resistance training for the legs. Sessions were supervised, and loads were set high relative to each person's ability and raised as they got stronger. Nine people completed the program.

#### What it found

- Leg strength increased by an average of 174%

- Mid-thigh muscle area increased by 9%

- Tandem gait speed, a walking test, improved by 48%

 At the start, weaker legs went with slower walking, and more muscle went with more strength. The authors concluded that high-resistance training produces significant gains in strength, muscle size and mobility in frail people up to 96 years of age.

 Keep it in proportion

 This was a study of nine people with no comparison group. Its importance is that it showed the response was possible at all, and larger trials since have confirmed that older adults gain strength with resistance training.

#### Why it matters

 Leg strength is tied to the things that keep people independent: rising from a chair, climbing stairs and recovering from a stumble. Muscle that is not used shrinks at any age, and muscle that is trained responds at any age.

#### How to start safely

- Begin with supervision from a physical therapist or a trainer experienced with older adults

- Start with sit-to-stands from a chair, step-ups and resistance bands

- Train two or three days a week with a rest day between

- Increase the load gradually once a movement feels easy
