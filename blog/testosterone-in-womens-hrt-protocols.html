Testosterone in Women's HRT: the overlooked hormone

Testosterone is a normal female hormone that declines with age, yet most menopause clinics still won't prescribe it. Here's what the evidence actually says — and where the gap between the science and the standard of care sits in 2026.

Ask most women on hormone replacement what's in their protocol and you'll hear estradiol, progesterone, maybe a vaginal estrogen. Testosterone almost never makes the list — even though it's the most abundant sex hormone in a healthy young woman by mass, and even though a global consensus statement signed by every major menopause society on earth endorses it for a specific indication.

The gap between what the literature supports and what women are actually offered is one of the most frustrating stories in modern HRT. Here's where the evidence sits, what testosterone realistically does (and doesn't do), and why so many clinics still won't touch it.

Women make testosterone too — and then they don't

A woman in her 20s produces roughly three times more testosterone than estradiol on a daily basis. It's made in the ovaries and adrenals, and levels decline steadily from the mid-20s onward — by the time a woman reaches her 40s, her total testosterone is often about half what it was two decades earlier (Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen, Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2015).

Surgical menopause drops it further and faster. Removing the ovaries removes roughly 50% of circulating testosterone essentially overnight.

Symptoms that track with this decline — in the women who are symptomatic, which isn't all of them — include:

  • Low sexual desire and reduced arousal response
  • Blunted energy and drive
  • Reduced sense of well-being
  • Loss of lean mass and grip strength
  • Cognitive fog that doesn't fully resolve on estradiol alone

The caveat: serum testosterone levels correlate poorly with symptoms in individual women. This is part of why the field has been so slow — you can't just draw a level, see "low," and treat.

What the guidelines actually say — in plain English

In 2019, ten international societies including the International Menopause Society, the Endocrine Society, and the North American Menopause Society (now The Menopause Society) co-signed the Global Consensus Position Statement on the Use of Testosterone Therapy for Women (Davis et al., Climacteric 2019).

The statement's core conclusions:

  • Testosterone is effective for hypoactive sexual desire dysfunction (HSDD) in postmenopausal women.
  • Blood levels should be kept within the physiologic premenopausal range — this is replacement, not enhancement.
  • Data are insufficient to recommend testosterone for cognition, mood, bone, or body composition as standalone indications.
  • No approved female-dose testosterone product exists in most countries, including the US.

That last point is the trap. The guidelines endorse the therapy. The FDA has never approved a testosterone product for women. So prescribing it means either using a male product at a fraction of the dose, or using a compounded preparation.

The evidence for testosterone in postmenopausal HSDD is stronger than the evidence for most supplements sitting in the average medicine cabinet. It's a regulatory gap, not a science gap.

Why most clinics still won't prescribe it

Four reasons, in order of how often we hear them:

1. No FDA-approved product. Clinicians are trained to prescribe within labeled indications. Off-label prescribing is legal and common, but it requires the clinician to own the decision, document it, and follow the patient. Many won't.

2. Old fears about virilization and cardiovascular risk. At physiologic doses — roughly one-tenth of a male replacement dose — virilization (acne, hair growth, voice changes) is uncommon and reversible if caught early. The 2019 consensus found no signal for adverse cardiovascular or breast outcomes at physiologic dosing in trials up to 24 months. Longer-term safety data are still being built.

3. Monitoring inconvenience. Doing this correctly means periodic total testosterone levels to confirm you're in-range, plus a symptom check. That's more work than writing an estradiol patch and calling it done.

4. Discomfort with women's sexual health. Uncomfortable but true. Many primary care visits allot seven minutes. "How's your libido?" is not a question that fits in seven minutes, and neither is the follow-up.

{callout: The core point} Testosterone for women is not experimental — it's endorsed by every major menopause society for postmenopausal HSDD. The problem is that no FDA-approved female product exists, so most clinics avoid the conversation entirely.

What a real protocol looks like — tuned to your numbers

Before starting testosterone, the workup worth having includes:

  • Total testosterone (LC-MS/MS assay preferred — immunoassays are unreliable at female ranges)
  • SHBG, which drives free testosterone availability
  • Estradiol and progesterone status — testosterone works best when estradiol is already replaced
  • Complete metabolic panel, lipids, CBC
  • A conversation about symptoms that separates HSDD from relationship factors, sleep debt, and depression

If you choose to run bloodwork through us, those are the markers we look at first. Dosing published in international guidelines targets serum total testosterone in the mid-to-upper premenopausal range — typically achieved with a small daily transdermal dose that's a fraction of a standard male dose. Titration is done based on repeat labs at roughly 6–8 weeks, then symptom response.

Realistic expectations, based on the pooled trial data (Islam et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2019 meta-analysis of 46 trials, n=8,480):

  • ~1 additional satisfying sexual event per month vs placebo in postmenopausal women with HSDD
  • Improvements in desire, arousal, orgasm, and pleasure scores
  • No meaningful effect on cognition or bone at studied doses
  • Small increase in acne and body hair in a minority of women; no increase in serious adverse events over trial durations

That effect size is modest. It's also larger than what flibanserin or bremelanotide — the two FDA-approved HSDD drugs for premenopausal women — produce, with a cleaner side-effect profile.

The compounding question — and the honest trade-off

Because no female-dose FDA-approved product exists in the US, women who want testosterone therapy generally end up with either:

1. A male transdermal gel used at 1/10th the male dose (off-label, hard to dose accurately) 2. A compounded cream or troche dosed for women

Compounded medications are not FDA-approved as finished products, which is worth naming plainly. What you get in exchange is a formulation actually designed for a female physiologic dose, rather than trying to shave a pea of male gel and hope. Both routes are legitimate; the right one depends on what's available, what your clinician is comfortable prescribing, and what your labs show over time.

Who this isn't for

A few honest exclusions:

  • Premenopausal women with normal cycles — evidence base is thinner and the risk-benefit shifts.
  • Pregnancy or planning pregnancy — androgens are teratogenic.
  • Active hormone-sensitive cancer — needs oncology co-management.
  • Untreated severe acne or androgenic alopecia — relative caution.
  • Women whose main complaint is fatigue or brain fog, not libido — testosterone may help, but the primary evidence base is HSDD. Estradiol optimization, thyroid, iron, and sleep come first.

The bottom line

Testosterone is a female hormone. It declines with age. In postmenopausal women with distressing low desire, replacing it back into the physiologic range is supported by the strongest consensus statement the menopause field has produced this century. It is not a cure-all, the effect size is real but modest, and it needs to be dosed and monitored properly.

The reason most clinics don't offer it isn't that the science is weak. It's that there's no FDA-approved product, no marketing budget behind it, and no seven-minute version of the conversation. That's a solvable problem — and it starts with a clinician who's actually willing to have it.
