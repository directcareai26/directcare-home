## Waist-to-Height Ratio vs BMI: What Each Number Tells You

 BMI compares weight with height and cannot see where fat sits. Waist-to-height ratio adds that missing piece, and the usual target is a waist under half of height.

 BMI uses only height and weight, so it cannot separate muscle from fat or show where fat is carried. Waist-to-height ratio adds a measure of fat around the middle. UK guidance updated in 2022 suggests adults with a BMI under 35 aim to keep the waist below half of height.

 The scale gives one number. BMI turns that number into a ratio with height. Neither one says where the weight sits, and that turns out to matter for heart and metabolic health.

### What BMI measures, and what it misses

 Body mass index is weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. It is quick, cheap and useful across large groups of people, which is why it is still on almost every chart.

 Its limits are well known. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so a muscular person can land in the "overweight" band. It also cannot tell fat under the skin of the hips and thighs from fat carried around the abdomen. Two people with the same BMI can have very different amounts of abdominal fat.

 That second gap is the important one. Fat carried around the middle is more closely linked with type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease than fat carried elsewhere.

### What waist-to-height ratio adds

 Waist-to-height ratio is the waist measurement divided by height, in the same units. A 33-inch waist on a 66-inch (5'6") person is a ratio of 0.5.

 In 2022, England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) updated its obesity guidance to encourage adults with a BMI below 35 to use this check alongside BMI. The bands it uses are simple:

- 0.4 to 0.49: healthy central adiposity

- 0.5 to 0.59: increased central adiposity

- 0.6 or more: high central adiposity

 The plain-English version in the guidance is to keep the waist to less than half of height. The same boundary is used for men and women.

 The short version

 BMI describes how heavy someone is for their height. Waist-to-height ratio describes where the weight sits. They answer different questions, so the two are more useful together than either one alone.

### What the research found

 A 2012 systematic review and meta-analysis in Obesity Reviews pooled studies covering more than 300,000 adults. Waist-to-height ratio was a better screening tool than BMI, and somewhat better than waist circumference alone, for picking out adults with diabetes, high blood pressure, abnormal lipids and cardiovascular risk.

 An earlier systematic review in Nutrition Research Reviews looked at where the boundary should sit and concluded that 0.5 could work as a global cut-off across sexes and ethnic groups. A 2016 analysis in BMJ Open found that a group of adults with a "healthy" BMI still had a ratio of 0.5 or more, and that group carried higher cardiometabolic risk factors than BMI alone would suggest.

 These are screening studies. They show which measurement sorts people into higher and lower risk groups more accurately. They do not show that a particular ratio causes or prevents disease in any one person.

### How to measure it at home

 The NICE method takes about a minute and needs only a tape measure:

- Find the bottom of the ribs and the top of the hip bones.

- Wrap the tape around the middle, halfway between those two points. For many people this is just above the belly button.

- Breathe out normally and read the tape without pulling it tight.

- Divide the waist measurement by height, using the same units for both.

 The string version works without any math. A piece of string cut to a person's height and folded in half should fit around the waist. If the ends do not meet, the ratio is above 0.5.

 Trouser size is not a reliable stand-in. Waistbands sit lower than the measuring point and sizes vary by brand.

### Where the ratio falls short

 Waist-to-height ratio is a screening measurement, not a diagnosis. It is less accurate in a few situations:

- Adults with a BMI of 35 or above, where the guidance does not rely on it

- Pregnancy

- Children under two

- Conditions that enlarge the abdomen for reasons other than fat

 It also says nothing about blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol or family history. Those still need to be measured or asked about. A ratio above 0.5 is a reason to have that wider conversation with a clinician, and a ratio below 0.5 does not rule out other risks.

### What to do with the number

 A ratio is most useful as a trend. Measured the same way every few weeks, it can show change in abdominal fat that the scale hides, for example when someone is gaining muscle while losing fat.
